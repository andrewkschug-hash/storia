# La Casa delle Finestre (The House of Windows) (la-casa-delle-finestre)

## Chapter 1: The grid (La griglia)

Irene arrives in San Quirico sul Nera in the afternoon. The town is small. Irene looks for Via della Scuola on foot. The suitcase is heavy, but she walks. The street is narrow and there is little noise. Loredana is standing in front of the door of the house.

Good evening. Are you Irene Colombo? Yes. I’ve just arrived. Good. This is the house. You’re upstairs, in the attic. The stairs are on the left. I live on the floor below. If you need something, knock. The street is quiet, in the day and in the evening.

Irene goes up the stairs with the suitcase. She opens the door of the attic. The room is small, but there is a large window onto the street. There is a bed, a table, and a chair. Irene leaves the suitcase near the bed. Then she opens the window and looks out.

Opposite there is a large building. It has many windows. One above the other, one next to the other. It looks like a grid. What is that house opposite? It’s the middle school. It has been closed for two years. Nobody goes in. In the daytime the windows are dark. Irene looks at the school for a minute. She does not see anyone at the windows. Then she closes the window because it is chilly.

When it is evening, Irene is alone in the attic. She turns on a light in the house and eats something. Then she goes back to the window. Outside it was dark. The school looked empty. Nobody was walking in the street. Irene stays there and looks. There is no car. There is no voice.

While she is looking, she sees a light. There is a light in a window, on the second floor. It is not strong. But it is there. Irene has seen the light and she does not move. She waits a minute, then two. The light goes out. Then it comes on in another window. It is not the same window. It is on the same row.

Someone? Irene does not see anyone in the street. So she takes a notebook and a pen. She writes: first one window, then another. She writes the time as well. It is late. She waits again. Then she sees a third light. Three windows. Irene looks again. The three windows are on the same horizontal line. It is not a line up and down. It is a line to the right and to the left. She does not understand. But she writes that down too.

## Chapter 2: The closed school (La scuola chiusa)

This morning Irene opened the window. She looked at the school opposite for a minute. In the daytime the school looks empty. The doors are shut. The windows on the row are dark. Irene picked up the notebook from the table. Last night there was a light, then another, then a third. This morning there is no light. But she wrote down the time. So it is not a dream. The school used to be a school. Now it looks like only a closed building.

Irene goes down the stairs. Loredana is in the kitchen with the coffee. Good morning. Did you sleep? Yes, thank you. But I have a question. Is the school really empty? Loredana does not look at Irene. She speaks quickly, like a lesson. There was an earthquake. Two years ago. The school is not safe. You do not go in. Nobody goes in. Everyone says that. Irene listens and almost believes it. In the daytime this story seems true.

Irene goes out into the street. The sun is already high. She walks as far as the school. A woman goes by with a bag. Excuse me, the school is closed, right? The woman answers quickly. It’s empty. You do not go in. Nobody goes in. Then the woman walks away. The words are the same as Loredana’s. Irene stays a moment. Then she goes to the big door. The door is shut. Irene does not try to open it. The courtyard gate is shut as well.

There is a courtyard behind a gate. Irene looks at the courtyard. There is a man with grey hair. He has water in a can. He waters the plants by the wall. But the plants are dead. They are yellow and dry. The water falls on the dry earth and does no good. The man waters them again. If the school is empty, why the water? Irene almost still believes Loredana. But the man is there, in the daytime. The water is real. The dead plants are real.

Loredana is at the window downstairs. That’s Quinto. He was the caretaker. Irene does not speak to him. She looks up, toward last night’s row. Irene counts three dark windows. The three windows are dark. There is no light. Quinto raises his head. He looks at the same windows. He does not look at Irene. Then he takes the can and walks away. He says nothing to Irene.

## Chapter 3: The contract (Il contratto)

The next day Irene goes to the town hall. The town hall is in the square, not far from the school. This morning the school windows are dark again. Irene does not stop. She walks to the door of the town hall and goes in. She has a short contract. Today is the first day. From nine she has to be here.

The office is small and quiet. There is a woman behind a desk. She smiles. Good morning, Dottoressa Alme. Good morning, Irene. Please sit down. Irene sits down. She signs the contract on the desk. Alme keeps one copy. Irene keeps the other copy of the contract. I am the director, for these papers. The contract is for two months. Then we’ll see. You scan the old papers of the town hall. Including what is left from the school. Nothing important. Whatever is left, in short. Alme smiles again. Irene listens and says little. That’s fine. I work with papers. Perfect. This is her job, then.

The boxes are downstairs. There is also a scanner, on a table. Irene goes down with Alme. There is dust and there is little light. There are many folders and many boxes. Alme points to a box near the scanner. Start from here. School papers, old years. You scan them, that’s all. You do not have to read everything. Alme goes back upstairs, still with the smile. Irene stays alone with the boxes.

Irene opens the first folder. There are lists and simple sheets. Dates, classes, nothing odd. She puts the sheet on the scanner. She has scanned three folders. The work is slow, but it is clear. Almost an hour goes by. Then she sees another box, further back. It is not Alme’s box. There is a folder on top of the others. On the cover there is a line in black pen. Do not scan. Irene reads it twice. Her hand is on the folder. Should I open this folder? Should I ask Alme now? But Alme said: you scan them, that’s all. Irene does not open the folder. She puts it aside, closed. She takes another folder, an ordinary one. She scans again for twenty minutes. The ordinary papers are easy. The closed folder stays by the wall. Irene has finished three small boxes, not all of them.

When Irene comes back up, Alme is at the door. She smiles. All well downstairs? Yes. I saw a folder. It says: do not scan. Alme does not lose her smile. That one stays there. It is not for today. If you find anything odd, you come to me. Not to the others. At midday you may leave. Irene says yes. But she thinks of the closed folder, downstairs. She has not opened it.

## Chapter 4: The notebook (Il quaderno)

After work Irene is in the attic. The school opposite is closed, as in the daytime. Irene opens the notebook on the table. She does not want a story. She wants the numbers, as on the folders. She takes a pen and looks at the windows. She counts. Five windows per floor, opposite her. She draws the school: many squares, a grid. It is not beautiful. It is clear. Then she puts on the numbers. Second floor, from the left: one, two, three, four, five. Third floor: six, seven, eight, nine, ten. Monday night’s row is three, four, five. Irene knows this because on Monday she saw that line. She writes Monday at the top of the page. Irene writes the three numbers too.

When it is evening, Irene turns off the light in the room. She leaves only a small light on the table, for the notebook. She sits near the window. She waits. Outside the school looks empty again. Nobody is walking in the street. Irene looks at the clock. It is five to nine. She waits again. Then it is nine o’clock.

While she is looking, she sees a light. It is window three, on the second floor. Like Monday, Irene thinks. Irene writes: Tuesday, nine, window three. She waits. Then the light goes out. After two minutes another window comes on. It is window four. Still the same row. Irene writes: nine-oh-two, window four. Then, again, dark. Irene waits for window five. She looks at the row. Five stays dark. She waits a minute, then another. At nine ten there is a third light. Irene looks at the drawing. It is not window five. It is window eight, on the third floor. She writes: nine ten, window eight.

The light stays a little, then it goes out. Irene opens Monday’s page. Monday: first three, then four, then five. The whole row, the same floor. Tuesday: first three, then four, then eight. The order is not the same. The line is not the same. Irene compares the two drawings. First, then, after: the numbers do not match. On Monday window eight was dark. No light, the whole evening. On Tuesday the last light is right there. Irene does not go out. Irene does not understand why. But she writes the time, the number, the floor. She reads the times again: nine, nine-oh-two, nine ten. The third floor, on Monday, was all dark. On Tuesday, no. She closes the notebook. She does not have an answer. She only has two pages, with the numbers. Irene leaves the pen next to the closed notebook. Window eight stays on Tuesday’s page. The school is dark again.

## Chapter 5: Mimmo (Mimmo)

In the afternoon Irene comes back from the square. Near the school there are three young people. They have a phone and they are laughing. One is older than the others. He is nineteen, or so he says. He has a red jacket and talks loudly. Irene walks slowly, because she hears the words. The boy in the jacket talks to his friends. He says there is a ghost. In the school. At the windows. Irene listens because she too has seen a light. For a moment the kids’ story seems possible.

Irene stops. She almost believes it, for a moment. She has seen the lights too. Excuse me. What have you seen? The boy in the jacket smiles. A ghost. In the empty school. A light. I filmed it. My name is Mimmo. I film the school for fun. Look. The two friends laugh again. Irene does not laugh.

Mimmo gives her the phone. Irene watches the video. She watches it twice. There is the school, in daytime, a little dark. Then a light in a window, on the second floor. The light moves and then that’s all. The video is short. Irene almost believes it again. It looks like the same window as Monday. When did you make it? At night, of course. The ghost is there at night. The kids say yes.

Irene does not argue. She looks down, on the phone. There is the time of the video. There is also the day, small, under the picture. Irene reads it calmly. It is not night. It is afternoon. Ten past four. Irene shows the time to Mimmo. Mimmo looks and shrugs. The phone is wrong. Or it’s the ghost in the daytime. Better. The friends laugh. Irene gives the phone back. Thank you. She says nothing else. But now she knows one thing. That video is not the evening light. It is an afternoon game. Irene looks at the school now, at twenty past four. The windows are dark. No light, no video.

Irene goes home. She goes up to the attic and waits. When it is almost evening, she looks at the street again. Mimmo is not there. The kids are not there. The phone, the laughter, the ghost: nothing. Irene opens the notebook. She writes: Mimmo video, afternoon, ten past four. She waits at the window. Outside it grows dark. It is eight, then half past eight. The street is empty. Then, at nine, there is a light. Window three, second floor. It is not a video. It is not afternoon. Mimmo has gone. The light, though, is there all the same. Irene writes down the time. She does not write the word ghost. She writes only: nine, window three, like Monday.

## Chapter 6: Quinto (Quinto)

The next day, in the afternoon, Irene goes past the school. The courtyard gate is shut, as always. Quinto is there, inside. Irene is outside, in the street. Between them there is the gate. Today he does not have the watering can. He is not watering. He stands by the wall, with his hands in his pockets. The dead plants are still there. Irene stops. Good morning. You are Quinto, right? Quinto looks up. Yes. The caretaker. I still am, a little. For many years. Irene stays on the street side. She does not try the gate.

Irene does not ask many questions. It’s a nice day. It is not cold. Yes. There’s sun. There is little wind today. I live opposite, in the attic. Loredana told me. You work at the town hall, don’t you? Yes. A short contract. Quinto nods. The town is small. Everyone knows everything. Then he is silent. Today there is no water for the plants. Or he does not want to water them. You’re not watering today, Signor Quinto? Not today. That’s enough. Irene does not ask why.

Quinto takes his hands out of his pockets. There is a bunch of keys. Many keys, on a ring. Quinto counts them quickly, in a low voice. One, two, three, four… then he stops. Some are small. One is large. Irene looks at the keys, not at Quinto’s face. Do those keys open the school? Quinto holds the bunch in his hand. He points to the large key. This is the door. Wait. Then he moves his finger. He points to another key, smaller. No. This one. Then he changes again. The big door is this other one. The gate… this one, maybe. Irene listens. Three keys, three answers. She does not understand which key is the real one. Quinto puts the bunch back in his pocket quickly. The keys make a noise. Irene hears that noise.

And the upper floor? Quinto shakes his head. I don’t go upstairs. The classrooms, the corridors: no. Never. I don’t go. It isn’t my floor. There is no light, there is no need. The school has been closed for two years. Irene looks up, toward windows three, four, five. And eight, on the third floor. Today, in the daytime, they are dark. Quinto does not follow her gaze. He looks at the courtyard, or the wall, or nothing.

Irene says little. All right. Thank you. Goodbye. Quinto is already turning away. Then he speaks, toward the gate, not toward Irene. The school is empty. He does not look at her when he says it. Irene walks toward home. At home she opens the notebook. She writes: keys. Not upstairs. The school is empty? She closes the notebook.

## Chapter 7: The timetable (L'orario)

At the town hall Irene goes down to the boxes again. Today she works on the allowed papers, not on the other folder. The closed folder stays by the wall. Irene does not touch it. She opens one of Alme’s boxes and scans. Lists, dates, classes. Nothing odd. Then, at the back, she finds a different sheet. It is a photocopy, a little faint. Irene found it among old papers, not in the forbidden folder. At the top there is a word: timetable. Underneath: middle school, last year. Irene has read it twice. So she puts the sheet aside, for later. When Alme goes by in the corridor, Irene scans another list. Alme said: you do not have to read everything. But this is a timetable. It is a school paper.

At midday Irene leaves. She has put the photocopy among her papers, calmly. It is an old timetable, in an open box. At home she opens the notebook and the photocopy on the same table. She puts the photocopy on the left, the notebook on the right. The table is small. The two pages fit. When she looks at the timetable, she sees classes and hours. Monday, nine o’clock: class 1A, room two. Then, after that, class 1B, room three. Then class 2A, room four. Tuesday, nine o’clock: again 1A, room two. Then 1B, room three. Then, though, class 3C, room eight, third floor.

Irene takes the pen. While she looks at the drawing of the grid, she puts the room numbers. Room two: window three, second floor. Room three: window four. Room four: window five. Room eight: window eight, third floor. Then she opens the page of the lights. She looks at one page, then the other. Monday, on the timetable: room two, three, four. Monday, on the lights: three, then four, then five. Tuesday, on the timetable: room two, three, eight. Tuesday, on the lights: three, then four, then eight. The two pages coincide. Irene writes the numbers one under the other, without a sentence. Irene still does not know why. She does not know who carries the light. No name on the timetable, only classes and rooms.

Irene reads the sheet once more. In one cell there is a circle, in old pen. It is not her pen. The circle is on room two, Monday, nine o’clock. Irene looks at the drawing. Room two is window three. Window three is opposite the attic. Irene goes to the window and looks. Yes. That room is right opposite. Someone circled that room, years ago. Irene does not understand why. She only has a timetable, and the lights that return. She closes the notebook on top of the photocopy.

## Chapter 8: She is thinking of me (Lei pensa a me)

The two pages coincide. Irene still knows that. But the pages do not say whether someone sees her. In the evening she stands at the window, on purpose. Window three is opposite. It is room two, on the sheet. The notebook stays closed on the table, for now. Tonight she does not count first. She only looks. She has turned off the light in the room. Outside it is dark. Nobody is walking in the street. The school looks empty. Irene waits for nine o’clock. It is five to nine. Then nine. On the pages, at this hour, the first light was already there.

While she is looking, she sees a light. Window four, then window five. On the pages, Monday was three, four, five. Tonight the light does not start from three. Irene does not move. Then the light is at window three. Opposite. Irene waits for it to go on, as on the pages. The light does not go on. It stops. Irene remembers Monday’s numbers: three, four, five. She remembers Tuesday: three, four, eight. Tonight: four, five, then three. Then nothing. This pause is not on the sheet. The pages coincide on the rooms. Not on this waiting. Irene holds the curtain with one hand.

A minute. Two. Three, maybe. Irene counts slowly, without a voice. The light stays. It seems that someone is there. It seems that they are looking toward the attic. Maybe. Maybe not. Maybe the light is only still. If someone is there, they see me, Irene thinks. If nobody is there, it is only a light. The two pages do not answer. Irene takes a step back. Then another. She does not turn on her light. She does not knock on the glass. But she stays near the window. Her heart goes fast. Her hands almost do not. Nobody calls. Nobody speaks.

Then, after a long time, the light goes out. It does not go on. It does not come back. Window three is dark. Irene waits another five minutes. The light does not come back. Opposite, nothing. Did someone see her? She does not know. Irene does not know if the pause was for her. She opens the notebook. She writes: window three, it stops, then dark. She also writes: it is not on the pages. Then she writes: maybe for me. Then she erases the last three words. She leaves the pause. She does not write the why.

## Chapter 9: Yesterday (Ieri)

This morning Irene opened the notebook. The two pages still coincide: the rooms, the numbers. Yesterday the light did not follow those pages. It did four, five, then three. Then it stopped. She wrote it: it stops, then dark. It is not on the sheet. She erased maybe for me. That line is gone. The pause remains. A problem remains, not an answer. Usually, at nine, there is a light. Yesterday there was one, and it stopped. Irene writes a new line: tonight I check again. She puts the two timetable pages under this page. First they coincide. Then there is yesterday. Two facts, one under the other. She does not write someone. She does not write why. Then she went to the town hall, like every day.

Work was short, but she did the work. She scanned lists. Dates, names, nothing odd. The closed folder was still there, by the wall. Irene looked at it. She did not open it. Yesterday there was a pause. Today there is a folder. It is not the same thing. Alme smiled and said little. Irene did not mention the pause. She did not mention yesterday. Going home, she goes past the school in daylight. The windows are empty. There is no light. It is not needed, in the day. It looks empty, as everyone says.

When it is evening, Irene is at the window again, on purpose. She turned off the room light, like yesterday. She waits for nine. Then nine ten. Then nine twenty. If the pause was for her, tonight someone comes back. Or not. Irene does not choose. She only looks. Tonight there is no light. The school stays dark. All the windows are dark. Usually there is a light. Yesterday it stopped. Tonight nothing. So yesterday was not a timetable. Tonight is not a timetable. Irene waits another ten minutes. She counts, slowly, without a voice. Nobody walks. No light. No pause. She opens the notebook. She writes: tonight nothing. She does not write maybe. She does not erase yesterday. So she goes to bed late, with two different evenings in her head. If she was wrong yesterday, tonight shows it. If she was not wrong, tonight is another fact.

The morning after, Irene goes past the school, early. Near the gate there is something on the ground, in the gutter. It is a pack. Inside there were camping batteries. Now the pack is empty, or almost. Irene picks it up with two fingers. It is not old. There is no dust. There is no dry rain. There is a small price, and a recent date. Someone bought batteries this week. Irene looks at the windows. By day they are empty. At night, yesterday, there was a light. In the closed school the ordinary lights do not come on. So that light was not the school. It was a camping light, with batteries. It is not a video. It is not a ghost. It is a thing you buy. Last night there was no light. But the batteries are here. Irene puts the pack in her pocket. She does not go in. She does not call anyone. She looks at the shut gate, then goes to work. Mimmo has a video in the day. This pack is for the night. Two different stories. Irene keeps this one, in her pocket.

## Chapter 10: Do not scan (Non scansionare)

Today Irene is alone downstairs, with the boxes. Alme is upstairs, in the office. The scanner is on. First she does the allowed work. It is her job. She opens three ordinary folders. Inside there are lists, dates, stamps. She scans one page, then another. She counts. She writes the numbers on the town-hall sheet. Nothing odd. Papers that should stay together. She puts the three folders back in order, with the right numbers. The town hall wants a clean archive. Irene knows how to do this. Then she looks at the closed folder, by the wall. The tape is different. The paper is different. The line is still there: do not scan. The other folders have the same stamp. This one does not. The town-hall stamp is missing. The list number is missing. Someone kept it out of the ordinary work. I should leave it, she thinks. But this morning there were the batteries. I should tell Alme. But Alme said: you come to me. Irene waits a minute. Nobody comes down the stairs. Irene opens the folder.

Inside there is a report, not long. Three pages. At the top there is a name: Sara Neri, teacher. She wrote about cracks in the school, before the closure. The wall is not safe, the report says. The children, no. She also writes: before the closure, not after. In the town-hall lists the school has been closed for two years. This report is from before. Someone closed, and put the paper away. Irene reads the first page, then the second, slowly. There is a date, two years ago, or a little more. There is a signature. In the other boxes the school is closed, and that is all. Here the school is not only closed. Here there is a wall, and a fear. This paper is not with the others. It has not been scanned. Someone put it aside, on purpose. It is not right, Irene thinks. But it is only a folder. She opens the notebook. She copies the name. She copies: cracks. She copies the date. She also copies: do not scan. Not as an order. As a fact. She does not copy everything. She is not a lawyer. She is an archivist. She does not put the report on the scanner. She closes it. She leaves it where it was. She looks for Sara Neri in the other boxes, quickly, calmly. Lists. She does not find other photos of her. She does not find other reports. So it is not Sara’s archive. It is a hidden paper.

When she looks up, Alme is at the door. She smiles. She did not say her name, downstairs. How many folders, today? Three. The ordinary ones. She does not count the fourth. It is not in the three. It is not on the town-hall sheet. All well? Yes. Did you find anything odd? Alme speaks slowly, carefully. She looks at the boxes, not at the closed folder. Irene looks at the folder, then at Alme. No. It is the first time she says no, like this. A lie, small, clear. I should say it, she thinks. But she said no. Alme does not lose her smile. Good. Then upstairs, a coffee. Irene goes up. She drinks the coffee. The name stays downstairs, in the folder, and in the notebook. Alme talks about the contract, the days, the scanner. If there is anything odd, you come to me. She has already said it. She says it again. Irene says yes. She has already said no, downstairs. After the coffee Irene goes down again, for a minute. The folder is closed, as before. The tape is in its place. Nobody must see that she opened it. It is an archivist’s thought, and a liar’s. She goes back up. Alme is still in the office. She does not ask why Irene went down.

## Chapter 11: Sara (Sara)

In the afternoon Irene opens another allowed box. There are class photos, old years, names underneath. One photo is of the last class, two years ago, or a little more. Behind the students there is a teacher. She is not in the middle. She is to the side, near a window. Irene looks at the face for a long time. Then the window in the photo. She does not know which room it is. She only knows: a window, a school, a teacher. That window is not her attic. It is over there, in a classroom. Under the photo there is a small name: Sara Neri. She is the same person as in the folder. Almost the same date. Irene compares, in her head: date of the report, date of the photo. Close. First the paper. Then the class. Then away. Irene does not speak. She holds the photo with two hands. She looks at the other photos in the box. Earlier years, other faces, other classes. Sara Neri is only there at the end. Last class, last window. She was not always here. Then she was. Then they sent her away.

A man from the office goes by and sees the photo. It is not Alme. Ah, Neri. They said she exaggerated. She wrote. She stayed after the children. Then they sent her away. They also said she took papers home, the man says, already far off. Irene does not know if it is true. She keeps the sentence, like the other one. Irene looks up. Away, why? Irene hears her own voice: too much. Then it is too late. Because she exaggerated, the man says. The school was the school. That’s all. Then he takes a coffee and goes. Irene does not ask where she lives now. She does not ask about the light. She keeps three things: she wrote, she stayed, she exaggerated. Writing was her job, Irene thinks. Staying after the children, too. Exaggerated is the town’s word. It is not a date. It is not a crack. The report is there. The photo is there. The town’s sentence is there. She puts the photo in the box, calmly. She does not scan it. She writes in the notebook: teacher, last class, window in the photo. She does not write: she is the light. She does not know.

In the evening Irene walks home. She is not looking for Sara. She is going home. The stop is there, on the way. There is a bus stop, near the square, before the school. Irene stops because the light is red, not because she is looking. Then she sees the woman. She was already there, or she arrives now: Irene does not know. A woman is waiting. She has a large, stiff bag. Irene looks at the face, from the side, as in the photo. She looks like the woman in the photo. Maybe. The age, the hair, the side. Maybe not. It is evening, and there is little light. The woman does not look at Irene. She looks at the closed school, a moment, then at the road. It is not a signal. It is a look. Irene does not answer, because there is nothing to answer. The bus arrives. The woman gets on. She does not run. She does not speak. The bag looks heavy. Like paper, not like shopping. If she took papers home then, maybe she carries papers now. Maybe not. Irene is not sure. She does not say: it is her. She does not say: it is not her. She does not take the bus. She does not call. She watches the bus leave. At home she writes: maybe Sara. Then she adds: I don’t know. She also adds: bag like paper. She looks at the school, not at me. It is not proof. It is an evening. Irene knows that. She closes the notebook. She does not ask Loredana. Tomorrow there is the town hall again. Tomorrow the photo is in the box again. That she wrote was true: the report is there. That she stayed, that she exaggerated: this she does not know.

## Chapter 12: The second floor (Il secondo piano)

In the evening Irene does not go up to the attic at once. From the attic she only sees the facing grid: window three, room two. Tonight she wants to see more. She stays in the street, on this side, in front of the school. She has the notebook. She has the drawing of the grid. She holds it low, under the streetlight. She puts the drawing next to the building. The numbers and the windows must coincide from this side too. From here the grid is wider. From the attic you only see a piece. Irene moves the sheet. She looks for three. She finds it, opposite her house. She does not try the gate. She does not go in. She is on this side. The school is over there. She counts the floors: ground floor, first, second. She also counts the second-floor windows, from the left. Three is opposite her, at home. Four and five are further along. Tonight’s light is not on three. It is further, toward the right. On the drawing, on the second floor to the right, the teachers’ room is there, or so it seems. Window three is further left. Opposite the attic. At nine there is a light, on the second floor, to the right. It is not window three. It is not yesterday’s pause. It is another room, over there. So the light does not stay only where Irene can see it from home. Irene writes: second floor, teachers’ room, maybe. Not window three. She does not go up. She does not call. She only looks, from this side. From the attic, yesterday, the light was at three, opposite. From the street, tonight, the light is on the second floor, elsewhere. Two points of view. Two rooms. The person, if there is one, moves.

Then a second light moves, behind her. For a moment she thinks: it answers. Someone is coming. Then she understands. It is not the school. It is her house, on the stairs, on this side. Irene turns. She is an archivist with a finished contract. The bus leaves. The school stays. It looks empty. Someone was there. She is not, any more. She looks a moment at the town, then at the road. She leaves for real. The archive is not hers. She does not come back. She does not switch it on. She does not sell. She looks once, and that is enough. Then the engine starts, and she goes away from this small town.

# Una Lettera per Elena (A Letter for Elena) (lettera-per-elena)

## Chapter 1: The sugar (Lo zucchero)

Elena arrives in Marina di Brenta on her bike with cold hands. There is fog on the road and over Caffè Brenta. It is her first shift. Her heart beats hard. Bruna waits in front of the door with the keys. Good morning, Elena. You are on time.

Good morning. I am ready. Bruna opens and the smell of coffee comes out at once. Inside it is still quiet: chairs on the tables, lights off. Bruna turns on the lights and points to the counter. You work here. Machine, cups, sugar, register.

Elena puts on the new apron and nods. First the sugar. Always full. Elena fills the bowls and arranges the spoons. Near the sugar there is a low shelf. On the shelf there is a fat book, thick and a little dirty.

The cover is dark and without a title. What is that book? Bruna wipes the counter and does not answer at once. Later. After the customers. Now we open. Elena leaves it and prepares the cups.

At seven thirty the first customers come in. Elena serves a cappuccino and a short coffee. Bruna stays close and corrects only when needed. At the counter Elena talks easily: good morning, here you go, thanks. The words come without fear.

Between one order and the next, though, she looks at the shelf. The fat book sits there, still, next to the sugar. A table near the window wobbles when someone rests an elbow on it. Elena notices it and smiles to herself. In mid-morning the customers thin out.

Bruna goes to the storeroom and leaves Elena at the counter. Elena dries her hands and steps closer to the shelf. She opens the book carefully. Inside there are handwritten pages, with different inks. Elena turns two pages and finds a recent sentence.

This table wobbles. She reads the line twice and laughs quietly. It is not poetry. It is something true, like the table at the window. Under the line there is white space. Elena takes a pen from the counter.

For a moment she thinks of closing the book. Then she writes a single word. True. She closes the book and puts it back near the sugar. Her hands tremble a little.

Bruna returns from the storeroom with a sack of beans. Did you see the book? Elena blushes and looks at the sugar. Yes. Just a moment. Bruna nods and does not ask anything else.

The shift continues: coffees, cappuccinos, a sandwich. Elena works well, but the book stays in her head. At noon Bruna brings her a sandwich behind the counter. Eat standing up. The first day is like that. Elena eats quickly and looks outside, into the fog.

Marina di Brenta feels familiar and new at once. In the afternoon she keeps serving and does not talk about Padova. At the counter she is good. In the book, maybe, she is more true. Before closing she arranges the sugar and looks at the shelf. The fat book is there, closed, next to the bowls.

Bruna turns off one light. Tomorrow we continue. Elena goes out into the cold of Marina di Brenta. In her head she still repeats: this table wobbles. True.

## Chapter 2: Bruna (Bruna)

The second day in Marina di Brenta begins with little fog. Elena arrives at Caffè Brenta and finds Katia at the counter. Katia washes the cups with quick movements. Hi. I am Katia. You are Elena, right? Yes. Nice to meet you.

Katia hands her a cloth and points to the sugar. Bruna wants the sugar always full. Elena nods. She already knows where to stand. Bruna comes out of the storeroom with a notepad and a pen. Good morning. Today I explain something important to you.

Katia looks down, but she listens. Bruna walks to the shelf and touches the fat book. This book stays here. It is not for sale. Elena thinks of the word true from the evening before. First rule: it is not sold. Never.

Katia raises an eyebrow, as if she knows the story. Second rule: it is not taken home. It stays at the café. And if someone wants to read it outside? No. Here. Only here. Bruna opens the book and closes it again at once.

Third rule: you do not ask who writes. You do not look for the name. You do not point a finger. Bruna's voice is steady; her hands on the book are not. Understood? Yes. Not sold, not taken home, do not ask who.

Katia nods slowly and puts a cup away. Bruna stays still in front of the shelf a second too long. Her eyes grow shiny. She looks at the book and does not speak. Elena takes a step forward. Is everything all right?

Bruna shakes her head and wipes one eye. Yes. Just… leave it. It's nothing. She does not explain. She does not say why the book hurts her. Katia looks at Elena and shrugs: do not ask now. Bruna puts the book near the sugar and returns to the register.

Her voice returns to normal when a customer comes in. Elena makes a cappuccino. Katia takes a croissant. Work starts again and hides the scene from a minute earlier. But Elena does not forget the three rules and Bruna's shiny eyes. Between two orders Katia speaks quietly near the machine.

The book is old. Bruna cares about it. Do not ask questions today. All right. Elena would like to know who wrote this table wobbles. She would like to know if someone has read her true. But the third rule is clear: you do not ask who.

In mid-morning Bruna asks her to fill the sugar. Elena opens the bowls. The book is a few centimeters away. She does not open it. Not today, not in front of Bruna. In the afternoon the café is calmer. Bruna counts the money and does not talk about the book.

Elena washes the cups and repeats the rules in her head. Do not sell. Do not take home. Do not ask who. Before leaving Bruna turns off the lights at the counter. Elena, thank you. You are good with the customers. Thank you. See you tomorrow.

Katia closes the display case and puts on her jacket. Outside, Marina di Brenta is already in shadow. Elena walks and still thinks of Bruna's shiny eyes. She has no explanation. She has three rules and a book by the sugar. And she already knows that tomorrow she will look at the shelf again.

## Chapter 3: Tuesday (Martedì)

Tuesday morning Caffè Brenta opens early. Elena puts sugar in the bowls on the counter. Katia prepares the cups and checks the coffee machine. Outside there is fog over Marina di Brenta. A van stops in front of the café.

Bruna looks from the window and nods. It's Pietro. Tuesday. Pietro comes in with two boxes of coffee. He has big hands and his gaze down. He brings the smell of the van and the beans.

Good morning. Good morning. Bruna makes introductions near the counter. Elena, this is Pietro. He brings the coffee every Tuesday. Pietro, Elena has worked here for a few days.

Nice to meet you. Nice to meet you. Then there is a short, strange silence. Elena looks for an easy sentence. There's a lot of fog today.

Yes. A lot. Katia raises an eyebrow behind the counter. Pietro leaves the boxes near the storeroom door. Bruna signs a sheet on the counter. Do you want a coffee before you leave?

No, thanks. I have to go. They talk about the weather again, because they don't know what else. Maybe it will rain tomorrow. Maybe. On the lagoon it always changes. The air between them is dead.

Elena is good at the counter with customers. With Pietro the words are small and cold. She rearranges the sugar for no reason. He holds the van keys in his hand. Before leaving, Pietro looks toward the shelf.

He does not look at Elena. He looks at the thick book near the sugar. Only a second, but Elena sees it. Then he opens the door and greets Bruna. See you next Tuesday.

Yes. Tuesday. He leaves without making more noise. The van leaves into the fog. Elena stays at the counter with an empty cup in her hand. She talked about fog and rain.

Nothing true. Nothing alive. Katia washes a cup and speaks quietly. Nice conversation. Elena shrugs. He's shy.

Or maybe he's just in a hurry. In the afternoon the customers thin out. Elena opens the book on the shelf. There is no new line. There is still vero under questo tavolo balla.

Elena closes the book and puts it back near the sugar. She thinks of Pietro's glance at the shelf. Why did he look at the book and not at her? Bruna puts the new beans in the storeroom and returns to the counter. Tuesday is always like this: coffee, papers, and little else.

Elena nods and serves a customer with sugar. She would like to talk better, but she does not know how. Katia changes the subject and talks about a dirty table. That table near the window still wobbles. Elena goes to fix it with a piece of paper.

While she works, she thinks again of Pietro looking at the book. Maybe he was only looking for the sugar. Or maybe not. At the end of the shift Elena washes the last cups. The van is gone. Only Tuesday remains in her head.

She finds no answer. She goes back to the counter and makes another coffee. Tuesday ends like that: with fog outside and silence inside.

## Chapter 4: The second line (La seconda riga)

On Wednesday the café is full from the morning. Elena works at the counter without thinking too much. She makes cappuccinos, puts out sugar, smiles. Then, between two orders, she stops near the shelf. She opens the book with hands still damp.

There is a new line, written in black pen. Today I am afraid of silence. Elena reads it twice. The sentence is simple and true. It feels like she already knows it, inside.

She takes the pen from the counter. She wants to write little, like last time. Instead her hand runs. Me too. At the counter I talk easily with everyone.

At home silence is heavy. Sometimes I prefer sugar, cups, and the noise of the café. If I write here, I do not tremble. If I speak, I do. Elena looks at the full page.

She has written too much. She wants to erase it, but the ink is already dry. She closes the book and hurries back to the counter. A customer asks for a cappuccino without sugar. Elena smiles and works, but inside she is still on the book.

Katia notices her silence. Everything okay? Yes. Just thoughts. Bruna passes near the shelf and does not open the book. In the afternoon the customers thin out.

Elena wipes the counter twice. Then she secretly rereads her answer. It feels too personal. The other person's line was short. Hers is long and exposed.

She is afraid she said too much to a stranger. But she does not take the page away. She leaves the words where they are. In the evening Bruna counts the money near the register. Elena puts the sugar back and turns off a light.

The book stays on the shelf, closed and heavy. Elena goes out and walks in the fog of Marina di Brenta. She repeats the sentence in her head like a short song. Today I am afraid of silence. She is afraid too.

But at the counter nobody knows. Before writing, Elena listens to the noise of the counter. One coffee, two sugars, a table near the door. Then the new line stops her. She is afraid too, but usually she hides it with an easy sentence.

In the book she hides nothing. She also writes: in Padova I talked about work. Here I talk about everything and nothing. The real silence comes later, when I close the café. Bruna asks if she wants a croissant.

No, thanks. Just coffee. Elena drinks and thinks of the second line. She would like to know who is afraid of silence. But the rule is clear: you do not ask who. So she leaves the question shut in her chest.

And she leaves too many answers on paper. Only the book. Only that second line. Elena thinks: next time I will write less. She is not sure she can.

## Chapter 5: Katia (Katia)

On Thursday it rains lightly on Marina di Brenta. Caffè Brenta has few customers. Elena and Katia work at the counter without hurry. Katia dries a cup and talks without looking at Elena. You wrote in the book.

Elena stops with the sugar in her hand. It is not a strong question. It is a quiet sentence. Maybe. Katia smiles a little. Everyone writes.

Nobody admits it. Elena puts sugar in a bowl and breathes. Bruna too? Bruna watches. She doesn't always write. Sometimes she only reads.

And you? Katia shakes her head and puts the cup on the shelf. I have never written. Why? Because if I write it, then it is true.

Elena understands at once. For her the book is easier than the voice. For Katia it is the opposite: paper is scary. Bruna comes out of the storeroom with a bag of coffee. What are you talking about?

About the counter. About the sugar. Elena does not correct the small lie. In the afternoon Elena rereads some pages of the book. There are different hands and different inks. Some sentences are practical: the table, the coffee, Tuesday.

Others are fear and loneliness. Nobody signs. Nobody says their name. Elena thinks of Katia, who sees everything and says little. She also thinks of herself, who writes too much. In the evening they close the café together.

Katia turns off the lights near the shelf. The book stays in its place, near the sugar. Elena? Yes. Don't ask who writes.

I know. It's one of Bruna's rules. Katia puts on her jacket and opens the door. It's not only a rule. It's also protection. They go out together under the light rain.

Elena does not say that she is afraid of silence too. She has already written it in the book, where nobody admits. Katia walks beside her without asking more questions. Elena lines up the sugar packets on the counter. Katia still talks about the book as something normal and dangerous.

Once a customer asked if it was for sale. Bruna said no. Period. Elena laughs softly. I would not sell it. Of course. Because you wrote in it.

Elena neither confirms nor denies. She looks at the shelf and changes the subject. Does the table still wobble? Yes. Like people. In the afternoon a little sun comes through the rain.

Elena thinks that Katia knows the café better than she does. She also knows the silences. That is why she does not write: she does not want to leave proof. At the counter they are colleagues. In the book Elena is alone with a voice without a face.

And Katia, who never writes, already knows too much.

## Chapter 6: Mirella (Mirella)

On Sunday Elena is at her mother's house. Mirella's kitchen is small and warm. There is a smell of coffee and bread. Mirella puts two cups on the table and the sugar nearby. You're staying here, right?

Elena looks at the sugar and not at her mother. I work at Caffè Brenta. For now, yes. Padova is over. Yes. The job there is finished. Mirella nods and fills the cups.

Here you have a home. You have me. You have the café. Elena smiles, but she is tired of this sweet pressure. I like the café. I like the counter. At the counter you talk a lot. At home you talk little.

Elena drinks the coffee and feels the kitchen silence. At the counter it is easier. Mirella looks out the window, toward the fog. Your father spoke like that, on paper. Elena raises her eyes.

On paper? Yes. He wrote notes. He wrote short sentences. In speech he was more closed. Elena stays quiet for a long moment. She thinks of the book near the sugar at the café.

She thinks of her long answers and the fear of silence. Mum, I'm not dad. I know. But sometimes you resemble him. After lunch Elena says she has to go.

Where? To the café. I have to help Bruna with the counter. It's Sunday. I know. Just an hour. Mirella does not fully believe her, but she does not stop her daughter.

Elena bikes toward Caffè Brenta with the wind in her face. The café is closed. The door is dark. She looks at the lock and laughs at herself. She used the café as an excuse. She is afraid of staying too long in Mirella's kitchen.

In the kitchen the words are true and heavy. In the book the words are true in another way, safer. Elena goes home slowly, without saying where she really was. In the evening she writes a message to Bruna. Tomorrow I'll arrive early at the counter.

Then she thinks of her father and of paper. Mirella cuts the bread and talks again about staying. Marina di Brenta is not Padova, but it is home. Elena knows it is true and that it is not enough. At the café I have a counter. Here I have questions.

Questions are fine. If they stay in the kitchen, though, they are heavy. Mirella touches her hand. Your father put true things in notes. At the table he said little.

Elena drinks the last sip of coffee. Maybe I do that too. I know. When Elena returns from the café's closed door, her hands are cold. She ran from one truth toward another.

And neither is said out loud. And of the silence she is still afraid to say out loud. Mirella wants her to stay. Elena stays, but she hides the true part in someone else's book.

## Chapter 7: Pietro again (Pietro di nuovo)

Tuesday again. Elena waits for the van. She tries true sentences in her head, like in the book. She wants to be the note: true, simple, brave. Pietro comes in with a sack of coffee beans. Good morning.

Good morning. Elena breathes and tries. Today... today at the counter there is a lot of noise. Yes. That is not what she wanted to say.

She wanted to say: today I am afraid of silence. But the sentence stays in her chest and does not come out. The weather? Fog. As always on Tuesday. Katia whispers behind the counter.

Brilliant. Elena blushes and hates the counter for a second. Pietro lifts the sack toward the storeroom. The sack opens near the sugar. The coffee beans fall on the floor with a small, ridiculous sound.

Oh no. Wait. I'll get the broom. Elena takes the broom from behind the counter. Pietro is on his knees and gathers the beans with his hands. Sorry. I'm clumsy.

It's fine. It happens. Even the table wobbles, here. Pietro raises his eyes for a moment and almost smiles. Bruna arrives from the storeroom and looks at the floor. Coffee on the floor is a shame. But then she laughs and shakes her head.

At least today you're not only talking about fog. Elena and Pietro laugh together. It is a short, clumsy, true laugh. It is better than last Tuesday's dead silence. When the floor is clean, Pietro signs the sheet on the counter.

Elena wants to say something true before he leaves. I like Tuesdays. The sentence comes out wrong and too small. Me too. The work. He thinks of work. She was thinking of him.

Pietro looks at the book shelf for a second. Then he takes the van keys and says goodbye. See you Tuesday. Elena stays with the broom in her hand. She has failed again in speech.

In the book she would have written better. Katia passes near her and speaks quietly. The beans were cute. Shut up. But Elena smiles too, despite everything.

In the afternoon she opens the book near the sugar. She does not write the bean scene. She only writes one honest, ugly line. Before Pietro arrives, Elena repeats a sentence at the empty counter. Today I am afraid of silence.

Her voice sounds strange outside the book. When he is there, the sentence disappears. Only fog, beans, and a broom remain. Bruna brings another sack of sugar onto the counter. Be careful, you two.

Yes. Pietro picks up one more bean under the book shelf. The book did not get dirty. Elena checks it. For a second she thinks of telling him: you always look there. She does not say it.

She only says thank you when he passes her the broom. Thank you. You're welcome. That is how the attempt ends: with two small words and many beans. Today I spoke badly.

Then she closes the book and goes back to the counter. Tuesday stays clumsy. But at least she laughed.

## Chapter 8: Sergio (Sergio)

On Friday Elena opens the book before the shift. The café is still quiet. The sugar is already on the counter. There is a new line, written in a hurry. My brother does not sleep. The house is full of quiet fear.

I stay awake with him and do not know what to say. Elena reads and imagines the wrong story. She thinks of a finished relationship. She thinks of an empty bed, arguments, a goodbye. In her head the brother is almost an ex, not a brother.

She writes a soft, out-of-place answer. Sometimes people leave. It is not your fault if the silence remains. Time helps, even if it does not seem so now. She closes the book satisfied.

She believes she has understood and helped. During the shift Bruna talks about a late delivery. Pietro was late twice this week. He has family problems, I think. Elena raises her head from the counter.

What problems? I don't know. I don't ask everything. It's not my style. Katia washes the glasses and talks without looking. At the counter you hear everything and nothing. Elena thinks of the brother who does not sleep.

In her head it is still a breakup. She does not connect Pietro to the book. She does not yet hear the name Sergio. In the afternoon she rereads her answer. It seems good. It also seems a bit out of place.

But she does not know that yet. A customer asks for sugar for the table near the window. Elena brings it to the table. The table still wobbles a little. Elena smiles at the memory of the book's first line.

In the evening Katia turns off the counter lights. Elena touches the book's cover near the sugar. She hopes the person reads and feels better. She does not know that Sergio really exists. She does not know the brother is in recovery after an accident.

She only knows one sentence on paper and a wrong guess. She closes the café with Bruna and Katia. Outside there is fog. Inside the book remains. Elena walks home and repeats: my brother does not sleep. Elena rereads the brother line while making the first coffee.

She still imagines a woman who left. She imagines closed doors and unread messages. That is why she writes about people who leave. Katia asks if she wants to swap the Tuesday shift. No. Tuesday is fine for me.

Katia shrugs and takes sugar to a table. Bruna returns to the subject of Pietro only once. If he arrives late, do not make a fuss. He already has enough. Okay. Elena thinks of the book and her soft answer.

She hopes she has not done harm. She does not know she wrote to a different story. A story of recovery, not of goodbye. The counter stays full. The book stays closed. And the wrong guess stays comfortable until the next chapter.

She still thinks of a finished love. The truth is simpler and harder. But that comes later.

## Chapter 9: Yesterday I wrote too much (Ieri ho scritto troppo)

Saturday morning Elena arrives in Marina di Brenta with a tired face. She slept little, and she kept thinking about her long pages in the book. Yesterday I wrote too much. She says it to herself while turning the key of Caffè Brenta. Inside there is already the smell of coffee and warm croissants.

Bruna prepares the counter without hurry. Did you sleep? Little. Bruna looks at her for a second, then goes back to the plates. Katia arrives with her jacket still open and hair damp from fog.

You have the book face. What face? The face of someone who wrote and now feels shame. Elena does not answer and puts on her apron. During the shift she works well, almost better than usual.

She smiles at customers, counts the money, arranges the sugar in two jars. The counter is her safe stage: there she knows what to say. On paper, instead, she said too much and got the tone wrong. Around ten she is alone for a minute near the shelf. She opens the book even though she promised herself not to.

There is a new answer, written in dark ink. It is not a breakup. It is my brother. After the accident. Elena feels heat in her ears and in her hands.

She got everything wrong. Her soft answer, full of advice about empty beds and goodbyes, was out of place. She wants to close the book forever and leave it with the sugar. Instead she keeps reading, line after line, as if shame could disappear through reading. Shame does not stop the reading.

She takes the counter pen and writes little, this time. Sorry. I misunderstood. I hope your brother sleeps soon. She closes the book and stands still, with the pen still in her hand.

Bruna passes behind her and touches her shoulder lightly. If the book is heavy for you, you can not write. I know. But Elena also knows another thing, more uncomfortable. If she does not write, she waits for the next line anyway.

In the afternoon the café is full and noisy. Elena prefers this noise to the silence at home, where thoughts become long. She thinks of a brother who does not sleep, without knowing the name Sergio yet. She thinks of her wrong answer and the useless words she put on paper. Katia, while washing cups, speaks without looking at her.

It is not an exam, Elena. It is only a book. Elena nods, but she does not agree inside. For her it is not only a book: it is a voice without a face. In the evening, after closing, she walks a little toward the lagoon and stops early.

It is not a day for open water. It is a day for shame and for the book on the shelf. Walking back, she laughs bitterly under the fog. Before opening, she washes her hands and arranges the cups like every Saturday. Ordinary work keeps her standing when her head is full of paper.

A customer asks for a cappuccino without foam and she makes it without mistakes. Another asks for cane sugar, and Elena thinks of the book near those jars. She would like not to open those pages again, but her fingers already know the gesture. When she reads the correction about the brother, she closes her eyes for a second. She feels foolish, and also cruel without meaning to.

She treated a real wound like a finished love story. In the afternoon she washes glasses with water that is too hot and does not notice at once. Katia hands her a towel and asks no new questions. Elena counts the till money twice, to be safe. In the evening she turns off the lights one by one and leaves the shelf for last.

She looks at the dark cover and thinks: tomorrow again. Shame walks home with her, but it does not close the book's door on her. In bed she rereads in her mind the three short lines she left: sorry, I misunderstood, I hope. They are few words, and they still seem both too many and too few. At the counter I am good.

On paper sometimes not. And yet she already knows that tomorrow she will open the book again, even if yesterday she wrote too much.

## Chapter 10: Bruna tells (Bruna racconta)

Monday evening Caffè Brenta is almost empty. Katia has already put on her jacket and said goodbye from the door. Elena wipes the counter with slow movements. Bruna stays near the shelf, one hand on the book's cover. Elena.

Yes. My husband died four years ago. Elena leaves the cloth on the wood and stays silent for a second. I'm sorry. Bruna nods, as if that sentence were a habitual gesture.

He was shy. At the counter he worked well, knew the names, made the right coffees. But some things he did not say out loud. He wrote them. That is how the book was born, near the sugar, with no price.

Elena listens without interrupting, because this time Bruna wants to talk. Then others wrote. Shy people. People who stay if they can leave a sentence on paper. Does the book keep people?

Sometimes yes. Sometimes only for a season, and then it is enough. Bruna looks at the sugar jars and laughs softly, without joy. It seems strange, a book so close to the sugar. But for him it was a safe place.

Elena thinks of her long pages and Saturday's shame. She thinks of the fear of silence written by a stranger. It is not a game. The sentence falls heavy on the empty counter. I know.

If you write, you are saying something true. Even if you do not sign. Even if you think nobody sees you. Elena feels her throat tight and looks at the storeroom, where the lights are already off. And if the person is here, in the café?

Bruna shrugs, but her eyes stay on the book. Maybe. But don't ask who. It is still a rule, and Bruna repeats it like a short prayer. I keep the book because he was here.

And because others need to stay without shouting. Elena looks at the empty counter and the cups already clean. Thank you for telling me. Bruna puts the book back in place, upright, like something sacred and ordinary at once. Tomorrow is Tuesday.

Pietro arrives with the boxes. Elena feels a jump in her chest and does not yet know why. Yes. Tuesday. She does not yet connect the van to the book, but something has shifted.

The book is no longer a private game between two pens. Elena spent the day not looking at the shelf too much. She served coffee, listened to customers, laughed at Katia's jokes. But when they were left alone, Bruna chose to speak for real. Elena feels the weight of a private story in a public place.

She imagines Bruna's husband behind the same counter, shy and precise. She imagines the first page, maybe ugly, maybe practical, like the wobbling table. Bruna keeps speaking softly, as if the words could break. I do not want a museum. I want a place where shy people do not disappear.

Elena nods and feels her own long answers like an excess. She used the book like a game, even if she told herself no. Now Bruna's sentence stays under her skin. While they close, Elena brings the chairs inside and counts the lights turned off. Outside the fog covers the street of Marina di Brenta.

Bruna gives her the spare keys by mistake, then takes them back and laughs softly. Tomorrow you work, today you listen. Elena leaves with the sentence it is not a game still alive in her ears. She walks home and thinks of Tuesday, the van, the shelf. It is memory of a shy husband.

It is a place for those who speak better with paper. And Elena, while turning off the last light, knows she is one of them.

## Chapter 11: A sentence in voice (Una frase in voce)

Tuesday. Pietro's van arrives as always, a little late. Pietro comes in with the coffee boxes and hair damp from fog. In the café the fridge makes a loud, continuous noise, almost angry. Katia hits the fridge with the back of her hand. This monster never shuts up.

Bruna laughs from the storeroom. Pietro leaves the boxes near the counter and signs the delivery sheet. Then he tries a joke, as people do when the air is too quiet. Today I am afraid of silence. With this fridge, impossible.

Bruna laughs again. Katia rolls her eyes and says the joke is old. Elena does not laugh. She freezes with a cup in her hand, halfway between the counter and the shelf. The sentence is the same as in the book.

Same words. Same order. Today I am afraid of silence. Her heart beats hard, and the cup trembles a little on the saucer. In her head one question only, clear and dangerous: Pietro?

Elena. Everything okay? Yes. Her voice comes out small, almost broken. Pietro does not notice the freeze.

He checks the boxes, talks about the fridge again, asks if they should get it checked. You should get it checked. I know. It costs, though. Elena looks at the shelf, then the book near the sugar, then Pietro.

Two worlds in the same room: paper and voice. He waves goodbye and goes toward the van. See you Tuesday. Elena stays still until the engine starts. Katia watches her from the sink, with narrowed eyes.

Did you see a ghost? No. Elena puts the cup on the counter. Her hands tremble a little, and she hides them under the apron. In the afternoon, when the café slows, she opens the book again.

She rereads the old line, the one she knows by heart. Today I am afraid of silence. Now she also hears it in voice, said as a joke about the fridge. It is no longer only paper. It is a truth dropped by accident from the mouth of a quiet man.

Elena says nothing to Bruna. She says nothing to Katia, even though Katia has already seen everything. She closes the book carefully, as if she could also close the thought. But the thought stays. Before Pietro's arrival, Elena has already prepared the space for the boxes.

She talked about the weather with a customer, without thinking. She put the sugar in place twice, a nervous useless gesture. When Pietro comes in, everything seems normal: boxes, signature, smell of fresh coffee. Then the joke arrives, and the normal breaks. Elena feels blood in her temples and the fridge noise becomes distant.

She remembers the page, the ink, the night she answered too much. She remembers Bruna: it is not a game. And yet Pietro laughs, or almost, and talks about the fridge like a bad friend. After the van leaves, Elena washes the same cup three times. Katia whistles a song and then stops, because she sees the tension.

Bruna asks if coffee is missing in the storeroom, and Elena answers late. In the afternoon she writes sentences in her mind that she does not put on paper. Is it you? Why that sentence? Why today of all days?

At closing she counts the money and is off by two euros, then corrects it. She goes out into the cold and carries Pietro's voice with her, not only his lines. If it is you, Pietro, I have already answered you many times. And you do not know. And I, at the counter, still do not know how to say it out loud.

## Chapter 12: She does not say (Lei non dice)

On Wednesday Katia arrives early, before Bruna even opens the till. She closes the door and looks at Elena without smiling. Yesterday you froze. Elena arranges the sugar as if sugar could save her. The fridge was making noise.

Katia shakes her head. It wasn't the fridge. It was the sentence. Elena does not answer at once. She counts the seconds, as at the counter when she waits for the coffee to fall.

It's him, right? The question is clear, and Katia means Pietro without saying the name. Elena looks at the book on the shelf, closed and innocent. I don't know. It is a lie, and it stays in her mouth like a bitter coffee bean.

Katia hears it. Okay. She says nothing else in that moment. She respects the lie, but she does not believe it. Bruna comes in with the keys and a sack of flour.

Today we have many orders. The counter becomes full and noisy: cappuccinos, macchiatos, croissants, names. Elena works on autopilot, with the right smile and fast hands. Inside she repeats the lie like a new rule. I don't know.

But she knows. Pietro's voice brought the line out of the book, and it cannot be put back in. In the afternoon Katia washes cups in silence. Then she speaks one more time, in a low voice. If it is him, you can tell me.

Not to Bruna. To me. Elena shakes her head and keeps drying a glass that is already dry. I don't know. She repeats the same lie, smaller and harder.

Katia shrugs. Okay. Then you don't know. Elena hates the sound of her own voice when she lies. At the counter she says true things to customers: two sugars, one coffee, a table by the window.

With Katia she lies, and the lie weighs more than a sack of flour. In the evening she opens the book and does not write. She only looks at the pages, the different inks, the short sentences and the long ones. She is afraid of writing the name. She is afraid of saying the name.

So she does not say. And the lie stays between her and Katia, like a cup left on the counter after closing. After Katia's question, Elena works as if she could escape inside the orders. She makes five cappuccinos in a row and does not mess up the foam. She smiles at a tourist who asks for directions to the lagoon.

She tells the truth about the road and lies about the person. Bruna passes and says that today Elena is fast. Elena nods and hates how easy it is to seem normal. In mid-afternoon a glass slips from her and does not break. Katia catches it in midair and raises an eyebrow.

Careful. Elena whispers thanks and goes back to the sink. She would like to say: yes, it is him, I heard it, the sentence was his. Instead she repeats the lie inside, like a bad training. When Bruna goes to the storeroom, Katia does not push further.

The silence between the two of them is full of things unsaid. Elena thinks that lying to Katia is worse than writing too much. Because Katia saw, and paper has no eyes. At the end of the shift she turns off the coffee machine and stays a minute in the half-dark of the room. Then she leaves and takes the lie home, closed in the apron pocket.

Before turning off the light, Elena touches the book's cover. She thinks: I don't know, I don't know, I don't know. But the truth, under the lie, is already fixed like a nail.

## Chapter 13: The almost (Il vano)

On Thursday, after lunch, Caffè Brenta is quiet. Bruna is in the storeroom looking for a sack of sugar. Elena opens the book near the shelf. She takes the counter pen. In her head the sentence is ready, clear, dangerous.

Are you Pietro? She writes the S. Then she stops her hand, halfway through the letter. If she writes the name, the double world ends. Or maybe something harder begins: voice.

She remembers Bruna's rules and Monday's sentence: it is not a game. She crosses out the S with a clean line. She looks out through the shop window. There is fog toward the lagoon, low and gray. She writes something else, safer and less true.

Today I would like to see the lagoon. Even if I am afraid of the silence out there. Elena rereads and feels the weight of the choice. It is true and also false. True because the lagoon exists, and she wants it.

False because it is not what she wanted to ask. She closes the book before Bruna returns. Bruna returns with a sack of sugar on her shoulders. Did you write? Elena surprises herself with the answer.

Yes. Bruna does not ask what, and that is a gift and a punishment. After the shift Elena bikes toward the Brenta lagoon. The water is gray, and there is little wind. Elena gets off the bike and stands on the shore.

She listens. The lagoon's silence is not like the silence at home. Nor like the dead silence between her and Pietro at the counter. Here silence has space, and the water moves slowly. Elena speaks in a low voice, only for the water.

You are Pietro. She says it to the water, not to the book, not to Katia, not to him. Nobody answers. She goes back with cold hands and the truth still closed. In the book she left the lagoon.

In voice she left the name, but only where nobody could hear her. At home, in Mirella's kitchen, she eats in silence. Her mother does not push, this evening. Are you cold? A little.

Mirella puts a blanket on her shoulders and asks nothing else. In the morning Elena worked without opening the book. She counted the hours until after lunch, when the place becomes quiet. When she finally takes the pen, she feels her heart in her fingers. The S on the page already seems like a shout.

She crosses it out and thinks of Bruna, Katia, Tuesday, the fridge. Writing are you Pietro would be honest, and also a violence. So she chooses the lagoon, which is true but is not the question. After the shift she bikes with the wind against her face. She passes the closed Caffè Brenta and does not stop.

At the lagoon she stays longer than she had thought. She looks at the water and repeats the name a second time, even more softly. Pietro. The name without a question mark seems heavier. She goes home with damp shoes and small hunger.

Mirella has left soup on the stove and does not ask long questions. Elena eats and thinks that the futile attempt tastes like almost. Almost true. Almost said. Almost enough, and instead no.

Elena thinks: I almost wrote the truth. Almost is not enough. The futile attempt remains: a crossed-out S, a lagoon in place of a name.

## Chapter 14: The outing (Uscita)

Friday afternoon Bruna claps her hands on the counter. Tonight spritz. Everyone. Pietro too? Pietro too.

I told him yesterday, when he brought the coffee. Elena feels her stomach tight, as before an exam. In the evening they close Caffè Brenta and walk to a nearby bar, on the street of Marina di Brenta. There is noise, yellow lights, and little tables outside under light fog. Bruna orders four spritzes and talks to the barista as if she were still at her own counter.

Pietro arrives late, with the van parked a little far away. He sits far from Elena, almost by chance. Then Katia moves him with a practical gesture. Here. Free seat.

Now Pietro is next to Elena, and the small distance is worse than the large one. Worse. Everyone talks at once: Bruna talks about the counter and difficult customers. Katia makes jokes about the fridge and the finished sugar. Elena laughs at the right moment, as at work.

With Pietro she exchanges two dead sentences that say nothing. The spritz is good. Yes. Good. In the group they are worse than at the café, where at least work sits between them.

In the book they would be alive, full of fear and truth. Here they are two mute chairs next to an orange glass. Elena thinks of the crossed-out S and the lagoon, and says nothing of this. Pietro's phone vibrates on the table. He looks at the screen and becomes serious in a second.

I have to go. Already? Sergio. My brother. Elena hears the name for the first time, and the book comes back into her head all at once.

Sergio. The brother who does not sleep. The brother after the accident. I'm sorry. It's okay.

He's in recovery. But tonight he feels bad, and they are calling me. Pietro leaves half a spritz on the table and takes the van keys. He leaves quickly, without looking at Elena for long. Bruna sighs and turns the glass in her hands.

Poor guy. Katia looks at Elena, and Elena looks at the glass. She does not say: it's him. She does not say: I wrote about his brother without knowing the name. She drinks a bitter sip.

The outing was supposed to help, supposed to make them simpler together. It made everything clearer and farther. Before going out, Elena looks at herself in the café bathroom mirror. She fixes her hair and thinks that a spritz is not a book. At the bar they also order chips, and Bruna insists on paying.

Katia tells a story of a customer who wanted three sugars in his tea. Everyone laughs, Elena and Pietro included, but their laughs do not meet. Elena smells the aperitif and feels a closed stomach. She would like to ask about Sergio without seeming curious. She would like to say: I have read you, and she cannot.

When the phone vibrates, the table changes temperature. Pietro talks about recovery without useless details, and that makes everything more real. Elena understands that the pressure on him is not poetry. It is hours, hospital, lost sleep, a brother who must get better. After Pietro leaves, Bruna proposes another round and Katia says no.

That's enough. Elena leaves half the spritz and says goodbye in a calm voice. She walks home under yellow lights and repeats the name Sergio. Now the book has a face, a van, and a brother in recovery. And she is still afraid to speak, even after the spritz.

Sergio has a name. Pietro has a life outside Tuesday and outside the book. And Elena still has a lie in her mouth, and half a spritz she does not want to finish.

## Chapter 15: Hospital (Ospedale)

On Saturday Elena does not go to the café at once. She bikes to the hospital of Marina di Brenta, with her hands tight on the handlebars. She does not go in. She stays outside, near the entrance, where the air smells a little of disinfectant and old rain. She has brought a bag with two sandwiches and a bottle of water.

She does not know if it is needed. She only knows that in the book she wrote sorry, and that apologies on paper are no longer enough. She waits. She sees people leave with tired faces, and sees people enter with flowers and bags. She does not see Sergio.

Sergio is inside, in recovery, and she has no right to that room. After half an hour Pietro comes out. His jacket is crooked and his eyes are tired. When he sees Elena, he stops. He does not smile.

What are you doing here? Elena lifts the bag, like a small useless proof. I brought something. For you. Or for your brother, if needed.

Pietro shakes his head. You shouldn't have come. The voice is low, but angry. Elena feels the cold rise from her shoes. I only wanted...

She does not finish the sentence. Out here there is no counter, no book, no fridge joke. There is only care that is not ink, and Pietro refuses it. Sergio is better than yesterday. But it is a long recovery.

It is not a game, Elena. The sentence hits her, because Bruna said the same words about the book. I know. Pietro runs a hand through his hair and looks at the van, parked crooked. At the café it's fine.

Here no. Here it is family. Elena looks down at the bag. The sandwiches seem ridiculous, and the water seems little. Sorry.

I came anyway. Pietro takes the bag, but he does not thank her. Then he puts it back in her hands. Take it to the café. Or eat it yourself.

I can't have you here too, now. Elena nods, because Pietro's anger is clear and true. It is not novel anger. It is the anger of someone with little sleep and a brother in recovery. Okay.

I'm leaving. Pietro does not stop her. Elena bikes away, with the bag in the basket and eyes wet from the wind, or from something else. For the first time she brought care without ink. She told Bruna she would arrive late, without explaining the hospital.

Bruna raised an eyebrow and said okay. Outside the entrance Elena counts steps and minutes. She sees a nurse smoke quickly and then go back in. She sees a man talk on the phone and cry without sound. The bag feels heavy, and she thinks that real care is not bread.

It is presence, and Pietro does not want her presence here. When he says she should not have come, Elena does not argue. She understands the anger because she knows the pressure, at least a piece. Sergio is not dying: he is recovering, and recovery is slow and hard. This makes the scene more ordinary and more difficult.

There is no hero, there is a tired brother and a barista out of place. Elena bikes back with heavy legs. At Caffè Brenta Katia asks where she was, and Elena says outside. Outside? Outside.

She does not open the book that day. For the first time the paper seems too far from real life. And real life, outside the hospital, sent her away with anger. And for the first time that care came back, refused. Behind her, outside the hospital, Pietro stays alone with the van and with Sergio inside.

Ahead of her there is only the road toward the café, and a book that cannot repair this day.

## Chapter 16: The argument (Litigio)

Outside the hospital the wind is cold. Elena is still standing by the wall. Pietro has the van keys in his hand. He has not said thank you. He only said: you should not have come.

Elena does not leave. Inside Sergio is sleeping, maybe. Outside the two of them and the fog remain. Why are you angry? Because you came.

I only wanted to know if you were okay. Pietro shakes his head. It is not your problem. Elena hears something hard in his voice. She waited outside because she was worried.

She brought a bottle of water and does not know why. Now the water is still in her bag. Pietro looks at the hospital entrance. Inside there is Sergio. Outside there is a woman who knows his secret sentences.

This mix scares him. That is not true. You have already spoken to me about him. Pietro lifts his gaze. When?

In the book. The silence becomes long. A car passes slowly on the road. A nurse comes out and goes back in without looking at them. Pietro tightens his grip on the keys.

That is not talking. Elena takes a step forward. You write true things to me. I answer you. So you already talk to me.

Every week. With words you never use at the counter. Pietro laughs without joy. No. Writing is not talking.

Talking is here. With the voice. With the face. With the risk of getting it wrong in front of you. Elena feels heat in her ears.

Then why are you brave in the book? And at the counter you are not? Pietro looks at the ground. Because at the counter there are people. Because I stutter.

Because Sergio… The sentence does not finish. Elena waits. He does not continue. I do not want a life only on paper.

After the accident I wrote so I would not fall. But I cannot live forever like that. Elena opens her arms. And I do not want to lie to Katia. I do not want to pretend you are only Tuesday.

I do not want to love a shadow in the sugar. Pietro turns toward the van. Then do not read anymore. The sentence hurts. Elena stays still.

You started it. This table wobbles. Do you remember? Pietro closes his eyes for a second. Yes.

But that was a small thing. Then it became big. Too big for a book. Elena speaks more quietly. For me it was already life.

Maybe a wrong life. But it was the only one where I was honest. Pietro opens the van door. Then we have a problem. You call life what I call a hiding place.

I call talking what you call fear. Elena does not find an answer at once. The argument is this. Book against air. Paper against voice.

Two truths that do not match. Neither of them is completely wrong. Neither of them knows how to fix the distance. Pietro climbs onto the seat. Go home, Elena.

Do not stay here like a guard. She stays on the sidewalk. She would like to say sorry. She would like to say stay. She only says nothing.

The engine starts. The van moves away into the fog. Elena looks at the hospital entrance. She does not go in. She has no right to go in.

She only has an open argument. And a sentence she repeats while she walks. Writing is not talking. Maybe he is right. Maybe he is wrong.

She goes back to Marina di Brenta on foot. She passes the closed Caffè Brenta. She looks at the door and does not stop the bicycle of her mind. Her hands are cold. Her heart is full of unspoken words.

In the book she would have written better. Outside the book she argued badly. And that is the difference that hurts. At home she tells Mirella nothing. She washes her face with cold water.

She thinks of the counter, the book, the van. Near home she still hears the van noise in her head. She wonders if Sergio knows anything. She wonders if Bruna will be able to read the fight on her face. The night is long.

Elena does not write. She does not have the book. And even if she had it, she would be afraid of the paper. For the first time paper seems too easy to her. Too comfortable.

Too far from the hospital sidewalk. She falls asleep late, with Pietro's sentence still alive. Writing is not talking. Tomorrow she will still have to work. Tomorrow the counter will not wait for the argument.

She thinks: if talking is this, I do not know how to do it.

## Chapter 17: The closed book (Il libro chiuso)

The next day Bruna arrives before everyone. She takes the book from the shelf by the sugar. She puts it under the counter. Behind the boxes of napkins. Elena comes in and looks at the shelf.

The shelf is empty. There is only the sugar. Where is the book? Bruna dries a cup slowly. Closed.

For three days. Elena is left without words. Why? Because last night I saw you. You came back with red eyes.

And Pietro did not come to say hello. Bruna sets down the cup. The book is not a hiding place for arguments. Katia arrives and looks at the shelf. Ah.

So it is serious. Elena works badly all morning. She puts too much sugar in a cappuccino. She forgets an order. She looks at the empty shelf every ten minutes.

Her hand reaches for the pen out of habit. Then it stops. There is no page to open. On Tuesday Pietro brings the coffee. He comes in with the crates.

He looks at the shelf. He also looks for the book. Bruna watches him without speaking. Pietro signs the sheet. He says good morning in a flat voice.

Elena says good morning the same way. They do not talk about the argument. They do not talk about Sergio. They do not talk about the book. The counter stays full of work and silence.

When Pietro leaves, Katia whispers. They are both suffering. Elena does not deny it. In the afternoon she opens the drawer under the counter. She sees the corner of the book.

She does not touch it. Bruna said three days. Elena respects the rule, with difficulty. In the evening, at Mirella's house, she eats little. Did you argue with someone?

Elena looks at her plate. Yes. With a person or with a piece of paper? Mirella smiles without joy. Elena understands the reference to her father.

With both. Her mother does not push. She washes the dishes in silence. The second day is worse. Elena thinks sentences and has nowhere to put them.

I would like to tell you that I am wrong. I would like to tell you that you are wrong. I would like a page. Pietro, in the van, drives toward other bars. At the end of the round he stops the engine.

He takes a scrap of paper from the dashboard. He writes one line. Then he crosses it out. Without the book the sentence feels false. Or too true.

## Chapter 18: A letter (Una lettera)

The next morning Elena brings a sheet of paper from home. It is not a page of the book. It is real paper, folded twice. At the café Katia looks at her. What do you have there?

A letter. Katia raises an eyebrow. For whom? For me. And also for him.

Katia does not ask the name. She only smiles a little. Around eleven the café is quiet. Bruna is in the storeroom. Elena opens the sheet on the counter.

At the top she writes a strange title. A letter for Elena. It is a joke and also a truth. Because the letter is for her, if he answers. And because she must stop hiding.

Under the title she writes more slowly. I am Elena Marini. I work at the counter. I have read your sentences. I answered.

I argued with you outside the hospital. You said that writing is not talking. Maybe you are right. So I ask you one simple thing. Elena stops the pen.

Her heart beats hard. She writes the most important line. If you are Pietro, tell me at the counter. She rereads everything. There is no poetry.

There is a name. There is a request for voice. She signs at the bottom: Elena. She folds the sheet. She opens the book on the shelf.

She puts the letter inside, between two pages. She closes the book. She puts it back near the sugar. Bruna returns with a sack of flour. Did you write?

Yes. But not on the page. On a letter. Bruna looks at the shelf. All right.

A letter is already a step outside. In the afternoon Elena serves customers with her mind elsewhere. She checks the shelf without opening it. The book looks the same. But inside there is her name.

In the evening Mirella asks. Did you speak today? I wrote. Mirella sighs. On paper again?

Elena shakes her head. Yes, on paper. But I asked for voice. Her mother looks at her for a long time. Your father never asked for that.

## Chapter 19: At the counter (Al bancone)

Tuesday arrives with low fog. Pietro's van stops in front of the café. He comes in with two crates. He greets Bruna. He greets Katia.

He looks at Elena and then at the shelf. Elena feels her stomach tight. Pietro puts the crates in the storeroom. He returns to the counter to sign. His hands tremble a little.

Bruna goes to the kitchen. Katia washes glasses and pretends not to watch. Pietro opens the book. He finds the folded sheet. He reads it standing, near the sugar.

His face changes. It becomes red and serious at once. Elena waits. She does not speak. She has already written everything.

Now it is the voice's turn. Pietro refolds the letter. He puts it in his pocket. He comes closer to the counter. A customer comes in and asks for a coffee.

Elena makes it with precise hands. The customer pays and leaves. Space remains. Pietro opens his mouth. He closes his mouth.

Then he speaks like a brave idiot. I… I am Pietro. Elena looks at him. She already knows. But hearing it is another thing.

Pietro Baldo. It is me. The one from the book. Elena laughs. It is not a mean laugh.

It is relief. It is tenderness. It is the comedy of a truth said badly. I know. Pietro goes pale.

You knew? For a while. Since the fridge. Pietro brings a hand to his forehead. I am an idiot.

Elena nods, still smiling. A little. Katia from the sink says quietly. Finally. Bruna returns and understands at once.

She does not ask for details. She pours Pietro a coffee. Drink. Then talk better. Pietro drinks.

He looks at Elena over the cup. Outside the book it is harder. I know. But you said the name. At the counter.

As I asked you. Pietro sets down the cup. I do not know what to do now. Elena wipes the counter. We work.

Then, if you want, we talk in the back. It is not a movie. It is a Tuesday with fog and crates of coffee. Pietro nods. All right.

## Chapter 20: In the back (Dietro)

After the morning rush Bruna gives a nod. Katia holds the counter. Elena and Pietro go into the storeroom. The air smells of coffee and cardboard. There is little space.

Two shelves, a sack of sugar, a cold lamp. Pietro closes the door halfway. Not all the way. Elena appreciates this. It is not a dirty secret.

It is only privacy. Pietro looks at his shoes. Sorry about the hospital. I was scared. Elena nods.

Me too. I came without asking. Pietro lifts his eyes. Sergio is a little better. He sleeps more.

Elena listens. This time he speaks in voice. Not on paper. In the book it was easier. I wrote and disappeared.

Elena smiles without laughing. I read and invented a perfect person. Then at the counter there was you. Shy. True.

Different from the paper. Pietro stutters for a moment. I… would like… He stops. He breathes.

He starts again. I would like to try again. In voice. The sentence is short. It is clumsy.

It is true. Elena feels her eyes wet, but she does not cry. All right. Me too. I would like to try again without hiding behind the pages.

Pietro makes a half smile. I will not be good at it. Neither will I. At the counter I talk easily. With you, no.

Pietro looks at a coffee crate. We can make mistakes. We can still write sometimes. But not instead of voice. Elena holds out her hand.

For a clumsy handshake, only that. Pietro shakes it. The hands are warm and a little sweaty. Not perfect. True, yes.

Katia knocks on the doorframe. There is a line. Elena pulls back her hand. I'm coming. Pietro stays one second in the storeroom.

Then he comes out behind her. At the counter work starts again. Cappuccinos, sugar, change. Bruna looks at the two of them and says nothing. Bruna's silence is approval.

Before leaving Pietro speaks again, quietly. Thursday? Elena looks up. Your day is Tuesday. Pietro shrugs.

I can also mess up the calendar. Elena chuckles. Thursday, then. He says goodbye and goes to the van. In the storeroom the smell of coffee remains.

And the new sentence. I would like to try again, in voice. It is not elegant. It is enough. Elena makes another coffee.

## Chapter 21: The book remains (Il libro resta)

In the days after, the book stays on the shelf. Near the sugar, as always. Nobody hides it under the counter. Elena opens it Wednesday afternoon. She writes a short line.

Today I spoke in voice and it was not a disaster. She smiles while she writes. She is not hiding. She is telling. On Thursday Pietro arrives without crates.

Only with his hands in his pockets. He orders a coffee like a customer. Elena makes it for him. They talk about the weather. They talk about Sergio, a little.

They talk about a bad movie seen years ago. It is not poetry. It is practice. Then Pietro asks for the book. May I?

Elena hands it to him. He writes under her line. Me too. In voice it is strange. But I want to continue.

He signs with a small P. It is no longer anonymous. It is no longer a double game. Bruna sees everything from the counter. At the end of the shift she calls Elena.

Come a moment. They go near the shelf. Bruna touches the cover. My husband started this book. Elena already knows this, in part.

Bruna continues. It served shy people. It was not supposed to become a prison. Elena listens in silence. You two did well.

You brought the sentences outside. And the book remains. As a place, not as a secret. Elena feels a knot in her throat. Thank you.

Bruna shrugs. Do not thank me. Thank the fact that you stopped lying to Katia. Katia, from the sink, raises her voice. I heard that.

Approved by me too. Elena laughs. On the weekend Pietro sends a short message. Sergio ate. I am fine.

See you Tuesday, and maybe earlier. Elena answers with a photo of the counter. No filters. Only gray light and the shelf. On Monday she writes in the book again.

Not a long confession. A useful note. The table by the window still wobbles. We should fix it. Pietro, the next day, adds.

I will bring a piece of wood. Katia reads and comments. Now you are boring in a healthy way. Elena agrees. Boring and present.

## Chapter 22: Open (Aperto)

Thursday morning fog covers Marina di Brenta. Elena arrives by bicycle. She opens Caffè Brenta with Bruna. They turn on the lights. They arrange the cups.

The sugar is full. The book is on the shelf. Katia arrives with a scarf wet from fog. Today is not Tuesday. Elena knows what she means.

Around nine the van stops outside. Pietro comes in with a small crate. And with a piece of wood. For the table. Elena smiles.

Good morning. Good morning. It is not a long speech. It is a work Thursday. Pietro puts the wood under the table leg.

The table does not wobble anymore. Katia claps once. Miracle. Bruna serves the first customers. Elena makes cappuccinos.

Pietro waits his turn without hurry. When there is space, he talks. Sergio has physiotherapy today. I will go by later. Elena listens and answers.

If you want, after the shift I can come outside the hospital. Pietro thinks about it. Yes. But only outside. Like last time, without an argument.

Elena nods. Without an argument. The morning passes. Orders, clink of cups, fog on the windows. Pietro helps carry milk from the storeroom.

It is not his job. He does it anyway. Bruna puts an apron on him as a joke. He leaves it on for ten minutes. Then he takes it off, red in the face.

Elena finds him handsome in an ordinary way. Not wedding-material fantasy. Thursday-material. Mid-morning they open the book together. They write one line with two hands, almost.

Today the table does not wobble. We do, a little, but on our feet. Katia reads and shakes her head. Sentimental. Elena is not offended.

Outside the fog lifts slowly. A piece of street becomes visible. Two bicycles become visible. Marina di Brenta becomes clear again. Pietro has to leave again.

He has other deliveries. Before going out he stops at the counter. See you later. See you later. They announce nothing.

There is work. There is fog that goes away. There is a man who also comes on Thursday. There is a woman who says things at the counter. There is a book that stays open to whoever wants it.

Bruna counts the money in the drawer. Katia changes the music. Elena looks at the door after Pietro has gone out. The van disappears into the damp street. She goes back to the cups.

The shift continues. The story, for now, stays open. Like the book. Like the café door. Like a letter that found an answer in voice.

# Il Villaggio che non Esiste (The Village That Does Not Exist) (il-villaggio-che-non-esiste)

## Chapter 1: The wrong train (Il treno sbagliato)

Giada has been on the train to Trento for almost two hours. She is seventeen and going to her father's for the summer. Her mother stays in Bologna, with work and the quiet house. Her parents have not lived together for three years, and she knows it well. Giada keeps a suitcase that is small but heavy between her legs.

Inside there is also her mother's old bag, taken without asking. The train is slow, hot, and almost empty on this stretch. Outside there are green hills, few villages, and few people. Giada tries to write a message to her father on the phone. The phone has had no signal for many minutes, and she puts it away.

She closes her eyes only to rest a little, not to sleep. The regular noise of the train becomes sleep without her noticing. When she wakes up, the train is already braking with a long sound. A recorded voice says the name of a station, not very clearly. Giada does not hear the word clearly, but she stands up quickly anyway.

She takes the suitcase, her mother's bag, and gets off the train. On the platform there is no other passenger, only still air. The doors close and the train leaves at once, without waiting. Giada stays alone under a low roof of gray concrete. The halt is small: a bench, a bin, no ticket office.

There is no timetable on the wall and no voice that explains. She looks for a sign with the name of the place, near the tracks. She finds it a little crooked, with dark old paint. On the sign it says Collevento in letters still readable. A black line runs through the name, from left to right.

As if someone had crossed the village out of the world. Giada reads the sign three times, slowly, under her breath. Collevento is not Trento, and it is not on her list of stops. She looks right and left along the empty tracks. She sees no people, only tall grass, silence, and a narrow road.

The road climbs toward the houses; in the distance there is a thin line of smoke. Maybe there is a real village, not only a dead halt. The phone still shows zero signal, without excuses. Giada touches the black line with one finger and feels the dry paint. It is not a joke made last night by a boy.

She picks up the suitcase and the bag again and walks toward the climb. Behind her the train is already far away among the bends of the hills. Ahead there is only the crossed-out name and a quiet climb. Giada takes a deep breath and starts to climb, step after step. She still does not know where she is really going, this afternoon.

She only knows that this is not Trento station. And that someone, one day, wanted to erase Collevento from the sign.

## Chapter 2: It's there (C'è)

The road climbs between low houses of stone and light plaster. There are closed windows, hanging laundry, and little noise of people. From one house's chimney comes a thin line of gray, slow smoke. The village is small, but it is not abandoned: someone lives here. Giada smells warm bread and wood burning in a fireplace.

She walks slowly because the suitcase pulls her arm with every step. In a little square there is a fountain that makes little noise on the water. The water falls on the wet stone, always the same, without hurry. Near the fountain a wooden door is open onto the street. On the door someone has written Inn by hand, in dark letters.

Giada knocks twice and waits on the threshold with the suitcase. A woman comes out of the kitchen with a towel still in her hand. She has gray hair pulled back and strong hands of real work. At last. Giada stands still, surprised, still holding the suitcase.

Excuse me? I did not book any room. You have arrived. Come in, please. It is cool outside. The room has been ready since this morning. I did not call anyone and I do not know this village.

Elsa looks at Giada's old bag and nods slowly. It does not matter. The room is there, and today there is space for you. Inside the inn it is warm and smells of soup and bread. There is a dark wooden table and two chairs that do not match.

On the wall there are old photographs in simple, crooked frames. Elsa takes Giada up the narrow stairs, without many words. The room is small, clean, and already fully tidy. The bed is made with light sheets and a pillow in the middle. On the bedside table there are a bottle of water and a clean glass.

There is also a carefully folded towel on the edge of the bed. As if someone had been waiting for exactly that girl, for hours. What is your name, please? Elsa. I am Giada Rinaldi.

Elsa nods, but she does not repeat the name out loud. Eat later, if you are hungry. Rest a little first: the climb is tiring. Giada leaves the suitcase by the bed and opens the window. From the window she sees the street, the fountain, and the chimney smoke.

The village is there, with people who live, cook, and speak quietly. It is not only a forgotten halt on cold tracks. But something does not add up in Elsa's voice, too sure. She said at last, as if Giada were years late. Giada quietly closes the bedroom door and sits on the bed.

She listens to the sounds of the village: steps, a door, a distant voice. Inside, the room was ready before she knocked on the door.

## Chapter 3: Not grandmother (Nonna, no)

In the afternoon Giada comes down to the kitchen, still tired from the train. Elsa is making soup and cutting fresh bread on the wood. Sit down, eat something hot: you need it. Giada sits and holds her hands around the warm bowl. I have to go to Trento as soon as possible.

My father is waiting for me, and he still does not know where I am. Elsa stirs the pot without looking up at once. Anna used to say that. Who is Anna? I do not know her. Elsa does not answer at once; she turns down the heat slowly, as always.

I am not Anna. My name is Giada, and I am seventeen years old, no more. Elsa looks at her carefully, from face to hair to the bag on the floor. Seventeen. Yes, seventeen. I have to reach my father in Trento.

Anna was seventeen when she was here, too. She also always wanted to go to Trento, with the same hurry. I am not your granddaughter. I am nobody from this village, and I am not staying. Elsa puts the bread on the table and sits opposite her.

Eat. Then we talk calmly, without shouting. Giada eats because she is truly hungry after the long journey. The soup is good, simple, of vegetables and light broth. But her hands shake a little on the metal spoon.

I took the wrong train, that is all. I have to go back to the station and check the times, if there are any. Not today. Why not? It is still day, and I can walk. Because it is late to leave from here without a plan.

And because in this village things have an exact hour. Giada does not laugh; she waits for a better, clearer explanation. Elsa does not smile and does not joke: she keeps her eyes steady. Tomorrow morning look at the square. Then decide whether to stay one more night or leave.

I am leaving. Anna used to say that, with the same sure, young voice. Giada leaves the spoon in the bowl and looks up. I am not Anna, and I will not be for you. I say it clearly, without false anger and without useless fear.

Elsa washes a plate and speaks without looking her in the face. I know. But the face is the same, and the bag too, almost the same. Giada touches her own cheek, then the bag on the floor. She still does not understand the whole story, but she feels the weight of the name.

She only knows Elsa does not say grandmother, as in a fairy tale. She says Anna, like a living name that still lives in this house. And that is more frightening than a story invented for children. In the kitchen the smell of soup and still-warm bread remains. Giada thinks of Trento and of her father's voice that still does not know this halt.

## Chapter 4: The rules (Le regole)

In the evening Elsa calls Giada into the kitchen in a firm, low voice. There is also an old man sitting by the wooden table. His name is Tullio and he drinks hot tea from a dark thermos. There are rules, and you listen to all of them, without laughing at once. Rules about what, exactly?

Of the village, and of who stays here after sunset. First rule. After sunset you do not leave. You do not leave the village, and you do not take the dam road. Second rule.

You do not go into the forest, at night or by day without a good reason. Third rule. In the morning you look at the square, always, before deciding anything else. Giada laughs once, nervously, because the rules sound like a game. It sounds like a game for children, not a real law.

Tullio does not laugh and does not look up from the hot thermos. Elsa does not laugh; she keeps her hands still on the table. It is not a game. The hour is the hour, here, and it does not change for tourists. Why after sunset, exactly?

Because the road changes, and becomes water. Changes how? I still do not understand. Tullio drinks and does not explain further, for now: he leaves the doubt. And the forest? What is in the forest that is so serious? The forest is not for walking as at your home.

It is for remembering, and for staying away when it is not needed. Giada looks out of the kitchen window, toward the roofs. The sky is still light above the low houses of the village. And the square? Why look at it in the morning, every day? In the morning you look.

You see if something has been moved, or changed name on a door. Moved by whom? Elsa shrugs, but her voice stays serious and steady. By the people who stay and who have been waiting too long. Giada still thinks they are exaggerating, like tired adults.

But their faces are serious, without an easy smile and without excuses. I am staying only one night. Tomorrow I take a train and go to my father in Trento. If there is one. What do you mean, if there is one?

It means you listen to the rules, first of all. After sunset you do not leave. You do not go into the forest. In the morning you look at the square. Giada repeats the sentences in her head, one by one, clearly.

They are simple, too simple to be only a story. Outside the sun goes down slowly toward the dark hills. Tullio closes the thermos with a dry click and stands up. Tonight you do not leave. Giada does not laugh anymore, and that is already a small change.

In Giada's head the three rules remain like three closed doors. Outside, toward the square, the village seems quiet, but she is no longer sure.

## Chapter 5: Dad on the phone (Papà al telefono)

In the morning Giada looks for signal all through the village, street by street. Near the fountain the phone gets nothing, not even one bar. Elsa says to go up to the field behind the last houses of the village. The meadow is open, with short grass and a crooked wooden pole. There, near the pole, one bar of signal appears on the phone.

Then two bars, enough for a real call to her father. Giada calls her father and waits with a fast heart in her chest. Hello? Giada? Dad, it's me. I am fine, but I am not in Trento. Where are you now?

In Collevento. I took the wrong train; I fell asleep. Collevento? I do not know that name; it is not on my maps. There is a halt with the name crossed out on the sign, a black line. Take the next train and come to Trento, right away. There is no next train, Dad.

The halt is empty and there is no timetable on the concrete wall. Her father stays silent for a long moment, as if thinking. Ask someone from the place: there must be a way. I am asking. Here they say strange things, though. What things?

After sunset you do not leave. Her father makes a short sound, almost a tired sigh. It is not a movie, Giada. I know. But here they say it seriously, without laughing. Be careful and call when you have a train or a ride.

All right. I will call you as soon as I can. The call drops and the signal disappears at once in the field. Giada stays in the meadow and looks at the village below her, small. The houses look normal, with roofs, chimneys, and hanging laundry. She goes back to the inn with the phone still warm in her hand.

Elsa is washing glasses and does not look surprised about the call. Did you speak with someone? Yes, with my father. He says to take the next train to Trento. There isn't one.

There hasn't been one for years, for passengers, on this line. Then how do you leave from here, during the day? Elsa dries her hands on her apron and looks at the window. You leave when you can. Not when you want, and not after sunset.

Giada puts the phone in her pocket and stays standing in the kitchen. She has spoken with her father, and that is true and concrete. But she remains in Collevento, without a next train and without a timetable. The signal field already seems far away, behind the houses. And her father's words do not open the wet evening road.

In the kitchen Elsa goes back to washing, as if nothing had changed. Giada thinks of the signal field and of her father's voice, cut in half. Without a train and without a timetable, the village rules weigh more.

## Chapter 6: Sunset (Il tramonto)

Before sunset Giada leaves the inn without telling Elsa. She wants to see with her own eyes the road toward the valley and the dam. She walks past the square and past the last houses of the village. The road goes down between low trees and stones still dry. Below there are lights of a long, gray, closed building.

It looks like a workplace, not a house with families and children. The sun touches the hills and the air becomes colder on the skin. Giada hears a sound of water, far away at first, then nearer. The sound is like a door opening under the hill. Water comes onto the road as a low sheet and grows slowly.

It is not a natural river born from yesterday's rain. It is a surface that grows and covers the stones one after another. If she goes forward, the water wets her shoes and ankles. Giada stops and steps back two paces on the gravel. Behind her someone coughs quietly, like a warning.

It is Tullio, with the thermos under his arm and a steady look. Every evening. The hour is the hour. Who opens the water? The dam, still today.

Even though the lake was never finished, up there. Giada looks at the building below, with the lights still on. They are not windows of houses lit for dinner. They are lights of machines and of work corridors, cold and sharp. So it is not magic.

Tullio shrugs as if the word were of use to nobody. It is a clock. It opens and closes, opens and closes, for years. We stay above, where the road stays dry by day. The water rises to the ankles if she does not step back at once.

She steps back again and feels the cold of the water on her shoes. After sunset you do not leave. Now you understand why we say it without laughing. Giada looks at the road that an hour ago was still dry. Now it is a dark mirror under the sky that becomes night.

She cannot cross, and not because someone shouts a ban out loud. Because the water is there, physical and cold, and it closes the road. She goes back toward the village with Tullio, without many useless words. Behind them the water remains on the dam road, every evening. At the fixed hour, like a clock that does not ask permission.

Giada no longer laughs at Elsa's and Tullio's rules. She has seen the proof, and the proof is wet under her shoes. Near the hydroelectric building the lights stay on, cold and regular. Giada understands that the rule is not born from a story, but from a machine. She returns to the village with wet feet and a head full of practical questions.

## Chapter 7: Neri (Neri)

The next day Giada is in the square near the fountain, alone. A boy her age smokes and listens to music through headphones. He has a dark sweater, worn shoes, and a tired look. The music is in English, at low volume, almost a secret. You're the new one.

I am Giada. Neri. Born here, unfortunately. Do you want to leave Collevento? Always. But after sunset you do not leave, not even me, even if I hate staying.

And during the day? During the day you need a clear reason and a car that comes through. Someone who takes you out, beyond the evening water. I only have my legs and this quiet anger under the sweater. He pulls a folded map from his trouser pocket.

It is a modern map, with roads, numbers, and clear towns around. In the middle there is a white space without a name and without a point. No village symbol, no small italic label. Here. We should be here, on this new map.

But we are not there, for the map: we are a blank. Giada touches the white space with her fingernail, slowly. Collevento is not there. Exactly. We do not exist. Seriously? Even on this year's new maps?

Seriously. That is why I hate this place. Not because it is small. Because it is erased, as if it were supposed to become a lake and nothing more. Giada thinks again of the station sign, with the black line. The line on the name and now the white on the map: the same idea.

Are you staying long? No. I have to go to Trento to my father, not here. Everyone says they are going. Few really leave, when the hour of the water comes. Neri puts out the cigarette under his shoe and looks at the valley.

If you find a car, tell me. I am not looking for magic and not looking for ghosts in the forest. I am looking for a road that stays dry and an honest ride. Giada helps him fold the map again, carefully. The white space stays shut in the folds, but she has seen it.

She cannot forget that blank at the center of the new map. Neri is not a village legend and he is not a ghost. He is a boy who wants out, with his own real name and anger. Giada stays a moment in the square after he leaves. She looks at the fountain and thinks of the white space on the map.

The village is under her feet, even if the map says no. Neri does not ask her to stay and does not ask her to become anyone. He only asks for a ride, if one comes, and a dry road. Giada folds in her mind the white space of the map, like a fact. The village exists underfoot; on the map, no: that is Neri's wound.

## Chapter 8: The register (Il registro)

In the evening Giada stays alone in the kitchen for a long moment. Elsa is outside talking with Tullio near the door of the inn. On the table there is a thick book with a dark, worn cover. On the cover it says Register in letters faded by time. Giada opens it carefully, page after page, without false hurry.

There are names, ages, and dates from different years, written by hand. Some pages are yellow and a little damp at the edges of the sheet. Near the end she finds a line written by hand, still clear. Anna Rinaldi, 17 years old. The date is today's: day, month, and year, complete.

As if Anna had arrived right now, this evening. Giada touches the ink with her fingertip, slowly. It is not fresh; it is dry, dark, and old under the finger. Someone put today's date on an old line of the book. Or changed only the year, every summer, like a ritual.

Giada feels cold on her back, under her sweater, without wind. The surname is Rinaldi, like hers, letter for letter. Like her mother's, Anna, in Bologna, far away. Elsa comes in and sees the open book on the wooden table. You should not have opened it.

Why is my surname on this page? Because Anna was here. And why is the date today's, with old dry ink? Elsa closes the register and holds it against her chest a moment. Because every year we remember.

Do you remember or pretend it is still that summer? Elsa does not answer; she puts the book in a drawer and closes it. Put things away and go to sleep. Tomorrow look at the square, as I told you yesterday. Giada goes up to her room and writes on her phone without network.

Anna Rinaldi, 17. Today's date. Old ink. It is not magic; it is a ritual on the paper of the register. Or a mistake repeated until it looks like a law of the village. Giada looks at her mother's bag on the floor of the room. Inside there is still the familiar smell of home in Bologna.

Outside there is a village that writes that name again in the book. With today's date, and with fear of forgetting Anna. Giada turns off the light and does not sleep at once, eyes open. In her head one clear line remains, like a sign. Anna Rinaldi, seventeen years old, today.

And she, Giada, in the same room, with the same surname. But she is not Anna, and she does not want to become her for anyone. The closed drawer hides the register, but does not erase the line in memory. Giada repeats the surname Rinaldi and feels how tight it is, here. Tomorrow, Elsa says, one must look at the square: she will, but as Giada.

## Chapter 9: Yesterday (Ieri)

The morning after the register Giada goes out with the phone in her hand. She does not want to talk to anyone. She only wants to remember well what she sees. In the square there is little wind and a smell of wet wood. The fountain makes a small sound, always the same.

Giada raises the phone and photographs the fountain from the left. Then from the right. Then from the front, with the three benches in the same frame. She does not laugh. She works as if she were doing a school assignment.

She photographs the grocery shop sign. On the sign it says: ALIMENTARI ROSSI. She also photographs the door, because the handle is dark iron. She photographs the bread counter, closed, with a chair outside. She photographs the name on the chair: TULLIO, written in pencil.

Elsa comes out onto the inn threshold and watches her. What are you doing, Anna? Giada. My name is Giada. Elsa makes a small gesture with her hand, as if to apologize without apologizing.

What are you doing, Giada? I am taking photos. Why? Because I do not trust memory. Mine, and other people's too.

Elsa does not answer at once. She looks at the square as if she had known it for too long. Yesterday is already far away. Giada shoots again. She photographs the short bell tower of the chapel.

She photographs the wall where someone has put a pot of geraniums. She photographs the number on the house next door: seventeen. She also photographs the shadows, because shadows tell the time. Neri arrives from the dam road with his hands in his pockets. Are you playing tourist?

No. I am making an archive. Neri looks at the phone screen. Here things move. I know.

That is why I photograph today. So tomorrow I can compare. Neri shrugs. Go ahead and compare. Then tell me if it helps you sleep.

Giada does not smile. She keeps walking along the edges of the square. She photographs the bench under the tree. She photographs the bench near the fountain. She photographs the third bench, the crooked one.

She notes mentally: three benches, two green, one brown. She also notes: ROSSI sign, pot on the left, Tullio's chair outside. The sun rises and the shadows shorten. Giada feels hungry, but she does not go back in at once. She wants to finish the circuit before someone tells her to leave it alone.

Elsa stays on the threshold another minute. Then she goes back in without closing the door completely. Giada also photographs that half-open door. It is a small detail. But small details, yesterday, were already lied about in the register.

In the end she has forty-two photos. She reviews them one by one sitting on the edge of the fountain. None of them is beautiful. They are useful. That is enough for her.

When she goes back into the inn, Elsa has prepared bread and coffee. She eats in silence. The phone stays on the table, screen off, like evidence. She counts the steps from one corner to the other. She also counts the closed windows on the first floor.

She wants proof that yesterday existed like this, and not only in her head. In Bologna, after arguments, she photographed rooms to remember who had moved what. Here she does the same, but the argument is with a whole village. The phone makes a short sound with every shot. That sound becomes a rhythm, almost a secular prayer.

An old woman passes and shakes her head, without stopping. Giada does not ask why. She already knows that questions, in Collevento, cost slow answers. She also photographs the sky above the bell tower, useless and necessary. If tomorrow the sky is the same, at least she knows something remains.

If everything else changes, she will have today's numbers and names. This idea keeps her upright better than coffee. Neri, before leaving, only tells her: do not show them to everyone. Some people hate evidence. Giada puts the phone in her pocket like a useful secret.

She looks at the fountain one last time. Yesterday's water and today's water look the same. The benches, though, she no longer trusts. Outside, the square looks still. Giada thinks: yesterday ends only when I stop looking at it.

And she has not stopped yet.

## Chapter 10: Morning (Il mattino)

Giada wakes early, before coffee. She opens the phone and checks yesterday's photos. Then she goes out in pajamas and shoes, without a jacket. The square looks the same, and it is not. The shop sign no longer says ROSSI.

Now it says ALIMENTARI BIANCHI. Giada feels a hit in her stomach. She checks the photo: yesterday it was ROSSI, clear. Today it is Bianchi. The crooked bench is no longer near the fountain.

It is under the tree, where yesterday there was a green bench. The geranium pot has moved to the right. For a second Giada thinks of something impossible. She thinks the village changes by itself while they sleep. Then she hears a sound of wood against wall.

Behind the shop there is a ladder. At the top of the ladder is Elsa, with a new sign under her arm. Elsa has not seen Giada. She is removing screws with steady fingers. Giada stays still a moment, then speaks.

So it is not magic. Elsa jumps and almost falls. She comes down two rungs and looks at Giada like a caught thief. You were not supposed to get up so early. You change the names.

You move the benches. Elsa comes down to the ground and sets the sign down. The new sign still smells of paint. It is not a game. It is work.

Work for whom? For the inspection. Giada laughs without joy. Which inspection? The usual one.

The one that is supposed to arrive. Elsa points at the square with a tired gesture. If someone comes from the dam, they must find a tidy village. Right names. Clean counters.

Nothing that looks abandoned. Giada looks at the ROSSI sign on the ground, old and still good. And yesterday it was Rossi. Yesterday we were thinking of another list. Sometimes we change according to the papers we have.

Not according to the truth. Elsa does not deny it. Truth, here, is a private thing. Outside you need a village that looks possible. Giada feels cold on her arms.

It is not a ghost that moves things. They are people with ladders and screws. This should calm her. Instead it makes her angrier. You made me think of a nightmare.

It was only staging. Elsa folds the old sign and puts it under her arm. Staging is a big word. We call this survival. Giada looks at the moved bench.

Can I help put it back? Elsa shakes her head. No. You are not on the morning shift. The sentence is ordinary and hard.

Giada understands: she too is an object out of place in the staging. She goes back in to get dressed. Before closing the door, she photographs the Bianchi sign. Then she photographs Elsa picking up the ladder again. Elsa does not ask her to delete the photos.

Perhaps because she knows it would not be enough. Giada compares photo and reality three times, like a math assignment. The number seventeen on the house is still there. The fountain is also in the same place. The things that can be unscrewed change.

This observation seems crueler to her than a nightmare. A nightmare at least does not need ladders. Elsa, after being found out, speaks less and works more. She sets the bench with precise gestures, like someone used to it. Giada asks if Tullio also helps.

Sometimes yes. Sometimes no. It depends on the back and the day. Giada thinks of years of mornings like this. Years of new signs and old names mixed together.

She understands why yesterday Elsa said yesterday is already far away. Here yesterday is erased with a screwdriver. Before going back in, Giada touches the Bianchi sign with a finger. The paint is almost dry. Almost: a detail that gives away recent work.

In her head she repeats: not magic, staging. The repetition does not remove the anger, but gives it a shape. In the kitchen Giada drinks water, not coffee. Morning has removed the mystery and left the lie. Lies, at least, can be photographed.

## Chapter 11: The inspection (L'ispezione)

Toward mid-morning Tullio sits on the chair outside the bread counter. He has a thermos and an old newspaper folded in four. Giada stops in front of him with the photos still in her head. Elsa changes the signs for an inspection. Tullio does not look surprised.

Yes. She does it every week, in certain periods. When does this inspection arrive? Tullio unscrews the thermos and pours a little coffee into the cup. In autumn.

They used to say in autumn. Giada waits for the rest. Tullio drinks and looks at the dam road. They have never come. Never?

Never on time. Never as people who check houses and names. Sometimes a technician passes. He looks at the water, not at us. Giada sits on the edge of the fountain.

How long have you been waiting? Tullio counts on his fingers, then stops. Thirty years, more or less. I was already old when they promised the first time. Now I am only older.

Elsa comes out with a bucket and washes the inn threshold. She hears the conversation and does not stop it. Why do you still prepare everything? Tullio shrugs slowly. Because if you stop preparing, you admit you do not count.

And we want to count, at least on paper. Giada thinks of the register with today's date and the old ink. That too was proof for someone who does not arrive. The inspection was supposed to say whether the village stayed or went under water. Then the lake did not come.

The papers did. The papers erased Collevento without wetting it. Giada watches Elsa scrub the stone as if for an important guest. And if they never come? Elsa answers without raising her eyes.

Then we kept it clean for ourselves. It is not the worst answer. Tullio folds the newspaper. In autumn you hear the talk in the village again. They are coming.

This year for real. Then autumn passes. And winter starts again with ladders and signs. Giada feels a tiredness that is not hers. Thirty years of dress rehearsals without a show.

You know it is a lie. Yes. Long lies become habits. Habits keep people together. Until when?

Tullio looks toward the dam again, even though from here you see little. Until the water decides, or the papers, or we do. Elsa empties the bucket into a drain. You are not here to judge us. Giada does not answer at once.

It is true: she arrived by mistake, not to give lessons. But she cannot call this only survival. It is also a trap made of calendar. Tullio offers her the thermos. Drink.

The inspection is in no hurry. Giada drinks a bitter sip. I am. I am in a hurry to understand. Tullio nods once.

Giada asks if there is an official inspection letter. Tullio smiles with only one side of his mouth. There used to be. Then the paper got wet, or lost, or put in a drawer too deep. What remains is people's voice.

The voice says: autumn. Every year the same season, like a broken song. Elsa adds that in spring they repair the walls. In summer they paint the signs. In autumn they wait.

In winter they tell themselves next year will be different. Giada recognizes the rhythm of a family that hopes without tools. At her house, too, sometimes promises without dates were repeated. Here the promise is thirty years old and has a bucket. Tullio shows her the folded newspaper: news from another valley.

There is not a line about Collevento. If you are not written, for many you are not real. That is why we wash the stone. To look real on the day someone looks. Giada drinks again from the thermos and feels the iron of the rim.

The inspection, she understands, is the name they give institutional hope. Without that name, only bare habit would remain. Then ask the right papers, not only the signs. Giada looks at the square cleaned for no one. Autumn, here, is a season that never finishes arriving.

## Chapter 12: Anna's photo (La foto di Anna)

In the afternoon Elsa goes to the woods for small firewood and leaves the inn open. Giada stays inside, not to steal, but to search. She opens the drawer under the register. Inside there are invoices, rubber bands, a key without a label. Under the invoices there is an envelope without a stamp.

Giada opens it with steady hands. Inside there is a printed photograph, not digital. A girl of about seventeen looks at the camera. She has her hair pulled back and a light T-shirt. She has the same jaw as Giada.

The same way of keeping her mouth closed when she is angry. On the back of the photo, in pencil: Anna, summer 2007. Giada sits on the floor near the cabinet. She looks at the photo a long time, without crying. She is not entirely surprised.

The register had already said the name. But seeing the face is different. The face is proof you cannot move with a ladder. Elsa comes back with a bundle of branches and stops on the threshold. She has found Anna.

It is not a question. Giada raises the photo. This is my mother. I know. Then why do you call me by her name?

Elsa sets down the wood and dries her hands on her apron. Because you came in with the same bag. With the same age on your face. With the same way of looking at the door as if you already wanted to leave. Giada feels a clear anger rise.

I am not a return. I am not a copy. I am not your second chance. Elsa lowers her eyes to the photo. I was afraid of forgetting the details.

So I kept that paper. Giada laughs bitterly. You kept her face in a drawer. And when I arrived, you took mine. As if a face were village property.

Elsa does not defend herself well. Anna left without explaining everything. For years I thought one day she would take that room again. Then you got off the train. Giada grips the photo until she bends a corner.

My mother lives in Bologna. She has a job. She has a daughter she did not want to send here. Or maybe she did, and she did not tell me. That sentence stays hanging.

Elsa takes a step forward. Leave the photo. No. This face has already been stolen enough. I am putting it in my pocket, not in your drawer.

Elsa does not stop her. Perhaps because she knows she is wrong, or because she needs someone to take Anna away for real. Giada goes up to her room and closes the door. She sets the photo on the nightstand, next to the phone. She looks at the jaw on the paper and then touches her own.

Same line. Different story. That difference must stay clear. Outside, Elsa reopens the register and writes nothing. The girl in the photo does not smile.

She has the tired eyes of someone who has already decided something difficult. Giada looks in the face for her mother of today and finds her only in places. The jaw, yes. The rest is a young stranger. This distance hurts and helps.

It helps because it shows Anna is not a frozen myth. She is a person who changed after Collevento. Elsa, in the kitchen, breaks the branches into equal pieces. The sound of wood fills the wrong silences. Giada asks where the photo was taken.

In the square, near the fountain. Anna hated being photographed, but that day she said yes. Giada looks at the square again from the window. The fountain is the same. The girl is not.

And she does not want to become the third version of that yes. She puts the photo in a paper envelope found on the table. She writes on it, in pencil: not Anna, mother. Then she writes: Giada. The second name is a border.

Elsa sees the envelope and does not comment. Sometimes silence is the only excuse she has left. Giada thinks: I do not hate my mother for the face. I hate whoever uses a face as a key. And Collevento used hers without asking.

## Chapter 13: The forest (Il bosco)

The next day Giada does not ask permission. She takes a bottle of water and Anna's photo in her pocket. She goes out onto the hill road when the sun is high. The forest begins right after the last houses. The air changes: cooler, more closed.

She remembers the rule: you do not go into the forest. She also remembers that the rules, here, have physical reasons. She walks anyway. After ten minutes she finds a metal-mesh fence. The gate is closed with an old chain.

On the post there is a yellow sign bent by time. At first Giada reads the drawing as a skull. Then she comes closer. It is not a skull. It is a pictogram of ground giving way: a stylized figure sinking.

Underneath, in block letters: DANGER SINHOLES. Giada feels relief and fear together. Relief because there is no storybook monster. Fear because the ground can really open. She walks along the fence without climbing it.

She looks for a spot where the mesh is lower. Instead she finds a metal plaque fixed to two posts. The plaque is dirty, but the letters can be read. SURVEY TEAM 1978. Underneath there are names, one under the other.

Marco Bellini. Paola Greci. Stefano Mura. Luciano Ferri. Two surnames are ruined by rust.

Giada reads aloud, quietly. Not to call spirits. So as not to forget they were people with a job. Behind the plaque the ground is uneven, lower, with different grass. She understands: something collapsed here, years ago.

The memorial is not magic. It is technical memory and civic mourning. She hears steps on dry leaves. Neri arrives without running. They had told you no.

I know. Neri points at the yellow sign. It is not a story to scare children. The ground eats. It eats machines, tools, sometimes people.

Giada nods. Why does nobody explain it like this in the village? Neri raises an eyebrow. Because "sinkhole" scares tourists less than "cursed forest." And we are not tourists.

But sometimes short stories keep children away better than explanations. Giada looks again at the 1978 names. Were they here for the dam? For the lake that was supposed to come. They measured, drilled, mapped.

Then the ground answered badly. Neri does not add bloody details. There is no need. Giada photographs the plaque. She photographs the sinkhole pictogram.

She photographs the low spot behind the fence, from a distance. She does not cross the mesh. Respect and fear, together, are enough. Let's go back. Before afternoon the shadows lie about how deep the holes are.

Giada follows him onto the hill road. In her pocket Anna's photo knocks against the phone with every step. Before the fence Giada walks on hard earth and roots. She smells mushrooms and damp iron. No bird sings near the plaque.

Maybe it is chance, maybe it is ground that does not like weight. Giada does not invent stories on top of the facts. The facts are enough: names, year, danger printed in yellow. She reads "DANGER SINKHOLES" again the way you read a timetable. A timetable is not argued with: it is used.

Neri says that as a boy someone had told him about the forest monster. Then he saw the documents at the town hall, in a forgotten folder. There was no monster. There were surveys and crosses on the maps. Giada appreciates that he prefers the surveys.

She too has always preferred evidence to fairy tales. That is why she hates when adults use fairy tales for convenience. In front of the 1978 names she stays a minute in silence. She does not pray. She only remembers that every big project leaves human bills.

Then she also photographs the gate chain, locked well. Proof that the ban is not empty theater. It is a physical border, kept by people who know why. On the way back her legs are heavier. Not from effort: from the weight of new facts.

Two different proofs: a face, a list of technical dead. Neither is a chosen destiny. They are facts, and facts weigh.

## Chapter 14: Neri tells (Neri racconta)

On the dam road Neri walks one step ahead of Giada. He does not speak at once. He waits for the point where you can see the hydro building below. There he stops and points with his chin. That was supposed to be the plant.

And down there, where you see the wide riverbed, there was supposed to be the lake. Giada looks. There is no lake. There is grey earth, stones, a thin thread of water. They built pieces.

They ran tests. They opened and closed sluices on schedules. Then money, or geology, or politics, stopped. The project stayed half-done. Giada thinks of the water every evening on the road.

So sunset is not a spell. It is a company clock. They still open according to an old plan. Because it is easier to keep a procedure than to cancel it. Neri pulls from his pocket the folded map Giada has already seen.

He opens it on the warm guardrail. Here, look. There is a white blotch where Collevento should be. No name. No drawn roads.

Only generic green and a blue line for the river. On the new maps the future lake was already drawn. Then the lake did not arrive. But the village stayed inconvenient. So they left the white.

As if we did not exist. Giada touches the blotch with a finger. You exist. I am inside it. Neri folds a corner of the map.

Existing for us and existing for them is not the same. If we go back onto tourist maps, questions arrive. New projects arrive. Offers arrive for us to leave "in a civil way." Giada listens without interrupting.

Neri talks like someone who has repeated these sentences in his head many evenings. The elders decided something ugly and smart. Stay off. We do not exist to help them. We exist, if needed, only for ourselves.

The sentence stays in the warm air. Giada repeats it quietly, to keep it. We do not exist to help them. Neri nods. That is why Elsa washes thresholds for inspections that do not come.

That is why signs change according to old papers. It is a defense made of staging and silence. Giada looks at the building below: windows lit even by day, maybe service lights. They exist all right. Yes.

They have badges, high cars, schedules. We have a square that moves and a deleted name. You want to leave here. Neri does not deny it. I want a road that stays a road even after eight.

I want a point on the map that is not white. But if only I disappear, the village stays weaker. And they become stronger. Giada folds the map together with him. She understands better Collevento's quiet anger.

It is not folklore. It is administration and water and fear of being useful in the wrong place. They walk back toward the village without saying more for a while. Neri explains that the dam still has technicians on shift. They do not live in Collevento.

They go up and down with cars that do not fear shallow water. In the evening they open according to a protocol printed years ago. Nobody in the village has the power to stop that protocol. Giada asks why they do not protest in the city. Neri laughs shortly.

Protesting requires existing officially. We are a convenient administrative error. If we shout, we become a problem to solve quickly. And convenient problems are solved with suitcases and compensation. Giada looks again at the white blotch on the map.

She understands the sentence "we do not exist to help them" as a defense rule. Not as empty pride. As a way not to become useful at the wrong time. Neri refolds the map carefully, always on the same creases. That paper is truer than the sign in the square.

Because it does not try to look like a perfect village. It only shows the absence. Giada asks if he has ever written Collevento onto the map by hand. Once. Then I erased it.

I was afraid of helping the company without meaning to. The confession is small and heavy. They walk another stretch in silence, with the building below them like a lit clock. The wind carries a smell of warm concrete from the building. Giada puts Neri's sentence next to Anna's photo, in her head.

Two different ways of not being used.

## Chapter 15: The engineer (L'ingegnere)

The next day, around eleven, a high clean car arrives. It stops in the square as if the square were a normal parking lot. A man in a light shirt gets out, without a tie. He smiles before speaking. Good morning.

I am from the dam works office. Elsa comes out at once, with her hands still wet. Tullio stays on his chair and does not raise the newspaper. Neri appears from the corner and does not greet. Giada stays on the inn threshold.

The engineer looks at the faces as if counting pieces. What a pleasure to see Collevento so tidy again. Elsa does not thank him. The engineer keeps smiling. We are in a new evaluation phase.

There are support funds. Compensation for those who choose to move calmly. The word "calmly" sounds false in the quiet square. Tullio speaks without standing. The autumn inspection?

The engineer makes a vague gesture. Let's call it a technical visit. No drama. Only numbers and solutions. He looks at Giada a second too long.

She does not look away. Is she new? She is passing through. A guest. The engineer nods, satisfied with the category.

Better. Passers-through complicate less than residents. He leaves a thin folder on the little table outside the inn. Inside there are sheets with tables and a printed letter. Read it calmly.

There is no hurry today. There is hurry in the projects, though. The engineer smiles at Neri as at a clever boy. Projects have calendars. Villages have people.

We try to make them meet. Giada thinks: meeting often means crushing. The engineer walks an hour among the houses, speaks little, photographs walls and roofs. He does not photograph faces. He photographs proof that the village is inhabited.

Toward evening he returns to the square and shakes Elsa's hand. Thank you for the tidiness. It helps everyone. Elsa pulls her hand back at once. The sun goes down behind the hill.

Giada looks at the phone clock. It is almost water time. The engineer opens the door of the high car. Are you staying? He looks at her, surprised that she speaks.

I am not. I have a meeting downhill. He gets in, closes, leaves. The high car takes the dam road without hesitating. Ten minutes later, from the same road, comes the sound of rising water.

The roadway gets wet, then covered. Tullio pours coffee as every evening. See? The rules are for those who live here. Not for those with the right car and the right badge.

Neri spits on the ground, away from Elsa's feet. He smiles and pays. Then he leaves above the water. Giada looks at the folder on the little table. Nobody opens it at once.

It would open a door they have kept closed for years. Elsa brings it inside anyway and puts it near the register. Not on top. Nearby, like a dangerous and useful object. Giada stays watching the road become a shallow lake.

The engineer brought a smile and a paid exit. While the engineer walks around, Elsa adjusts a pot again. The gesture is automatic, from an imaginary inspection. Giada notices it and hates it. She also hates the man's smile, too smooth.

A meeting smile, not a wet-road smile. In the folder there are high numbers and low words: opportunity, support, orderly closure. Giada reads only the first lines and closes it. She does not want the numbers in her head too soon. The engineer asks Tullio about last winter.

Tullio answers with three words and a long look. Cold. As always. The man nods as if he had received a full report. Before getting into the car he checks the watch on his wrist.

He knows water time better than many residents. That is why he leaves on time. Not because he respects the rule: because the rule does not apply to him. It applies to him only if he stays on foot like the others. And he does not stay.

When the water arrives, Giada tries to imagine the high car already far away, dry. The image irritates her more than the sound of the current. Neri says that one day he too will have a car like that. Then he adds: or a real ticket office. Elsa does not comment.

She only looks at the road that can no longer be crossed. He also brought the clearest proof of the day. Collevento has rules. The company has exceptions.

## Chapter 16: Giada hates Elsa (Giada odia Elsa)

After dinner the inn is empty of guests. There is only Elsa drying glasses and Giada who does not help. The silence lasts too long. Giada breaks it with a badly prepared sentence. You did not want a guest.

Elsa stops the cloth. You wanted Anna. Elsa breathes through her nose. Do not start again. Giada raises her voice, not too much, enough.

The room was already ready. The register had her name with today's date. You called me Anna on the threshold. You kept her photo like a clue to use. Elsa sets the glass down too hard.

Your mother left. She left holes. I filled the holes as I could. Not with me. I am not putty.

I am not a second Anna to put in the made bed. Elsa blushes, not from shame alone: also from anger. You do not know what it means to wait for someone who does not write. Waiting for an inspection is one thing. Waiting for a girl who said "I'll come back" is another.

Giada feels Anna's photo in the hoodie pocket. My mother was seventeen. Me too. That does not give you rights over my face. Elsa takes a step toward her.

When you got off the train I saw a possibility. Not a person. A possibility. Elsa closes her eyes a second. Yes.

A possibility. I wanted Anna, not a guest. The confession is clear. There is no magic in it. There is only need, and need does damage.

Giada trembles in her hands. Then stop. Stop preparing my room like an altar. Stop using my wrong name. Stop washing the square to make me stay in an old story.

Elsa rests her hands on the counter. If I stop, only the emptiness remains. The emptiness was already there. You put me on top of it, like a rug. The two women look at each other without a table wide enough between them.

Outside, far off, the dam water makes its usual sound. Inside, the anger is higher than the water. Elsa speaks more quietly. I do not hate you. Giada shakes her head.

Right now I hate what you did. Maybe tomorrow I would hate less. Tonight, no. Elsa nods, accepting the blow. Sleep here anyway.

Not because you are Anna. Because outside, at night, the road does not forgive. Giada wants to refuse on principle. Then she thinks of the engineer's high car and the water. Principle does not keep legs dry.

All right. I sleep here. But I have not come back. Not for you. Elsa turns off the counter light.

I know. Now I know. Giada goes up the stairs without saying good night. In the room Anna's photo is still on the nightstand. During the fight Giada also lists the small things.

Coffee ready in the morning as for someone expected. The room without dust, too perfect for a chance guest. The way Elsa looks at her when she is silent. Every detail was a net. Elsa does not deny the details.

She only says that the wrong kind of love does not give notice. Giada refuses the word love for that trap. Call it need, then. Need does not make me Anna. Elsa sits on a low chair, suddenly old.

You are right. I wanted Anna. I wanted to close an account open for too many years. You entered that account without signing. Giada stays standing.

Then close the account without me. Close it with a photo, with a name, with the emptiness. Not with a living girl who has to go to Trento. Elsa passes a hand over her eyes. All right.

From tomorrow I will only call you Giada. Even if the mouth slips. Giada accepts the promise without thanking her. Promises, in Collevento, have a bad history. But this one, at least, is born from a real fight.

And a real fight is more honest than a room already made up. Giada turns it face down. Not to erase the mother. To remember that tonight she won, at least, her own name.

## Chapter 17: Call to Bologna (Chiamata a Bologna)

After the fight with Elsa, Giada does not stay in her room. She takes the phone and goes down again toward the field behind the houses. There, once, the signal worked with her father. She walks through the tall grass with shoes still damp from yesterday. The sun is low, but it is not sunset yet.

She checks the bars: one, then two, then three. She calls Bologna before her voice fails her. The phone rings for a long time, as if nobody wanted to answer. Then she hears her mother's voice, tired and precise. Giada?

Mom, it's me. I am fine, but I am not in Trento yet. Anna stays silent one second too long. Where are you? In Collevento.

The silence becomes longer, almost physical. Giada hears only the wind in the grass and the pulse in her neck. Mom? How did you get there? I took the wrong train.

I got off at a halt with the name crossed out. Anna takes a short breath, as if she had been hit. So you know. Know what? That that place still exists, even if nobody writes it on the maps.

Giada closes her eyes and holds the phone tighter. You were there. Yes. I was your age, and I carried that bag you took. Elsa calls me Anna.

She looks at my face as if I had come back. Do not return after eight. The sentence arrives dry, without a soft explanation. After eight? After sunset the road becomes water.

Everyone there knows it, and I knew it too. You knew and you never told me. Anna does not deny it; she does not invent a small excuse. I thought you would never go there. I thought it was enough to erase that name from our house.

Here your name is in the register, with today's date. And a photo of you at seventeen. Anna stays silent again, shorter this time. Then listen to me, not Elsa. Do not stay after eight.

If you go back on the road late, the water comes anyway. It is not a story to scare children. It is a dam clock, and they still do it. Giada looks toward the dam road, already in shadow. Why didn't you tell me when I left?

Because I was afraid you would go looking for it on purpose. Like a riddle to win. Giada smiles bitterly, because her mother saw her clearly. Now I am inside it. Then leave during the day.

Do not become the girl they tell about every summer. The signal drops to a single bar. Mom, wait. Do not return after eight. The line breaks before Giada can answer.

She stays with the warm phone against her ear and the empty field around her. Her mother knew about the water. She knew about the dam, the evening, the real rule. And she kept quiet for years, the way you keep quiet a difficult name. Giada looks at the time on the phone: sunset is near.

She goes back toward the village without running, but without stopping. It is no longer only a wrong train. It is a family story someone hid under silence. And now that story speaks to her in her mother's voice. Do not return after eight.

Giada repeats the sentence under her breath, like a new rule. Not Elsa's rule, and not the company's rule. Anna's rule, said late, but said clearly. Near the houses she smells dinner and damp wood. A distant radio broadcasts news from cities that exist on timetables.

Giada checks the time again: she still has time before the water. She walks more slowly, because now she knows hurry does not cancel the dam. She also knows her mother chose silence from fear, not from magic. That fear left Giada without a family map. Now the map is physical: road, sluice, sunset, eight.

She enters the inn without looking for Elsa for a second fight. She goes up, closes the door, and writes in the notes: mom knew. Underneath she writes: not after eight. Then she turns off the screen and listens to the village become evening. She is no longer angry only with Elsa.

She is also angry at Bologna's silence, and a little relieved. At least the water rule has a known voice. Anna's voice, which finally said the hard part.

## Chapter 18: Why (Perché)

Giada calls back at once, because one sentence is not enough. The signal holds, weak but present. Anna answers on the second ring, as if she were waiting. Why did you leave? Do not tell me only about the water.

Anna hesitates; you can hear a chair in Bologna. Because if I stayed I became the village girl. The one who returns every summer in everyone's mouth. There was a boy. Giada feels her chest tighten, but she does not interrupt.

A boy from Collevento. He was not an official boyfriend, and it was not a fairy tale. He was someone I stayed with too long in the square. Is he Neri's father? No.

The answer comes at once, with no room for tidy knots. Do not tidy the story to make it fit. That boy is not the father of anyone you know there. He left years later, or he stayed, I no longer know. That is not the point.

Then what is the point? The point is that Elsa wanted me to be the proof. Proof of what? That Collevento still had young people, still life, still a future. They wanted me to stay in the register, not only on the train.

Giada looks toward the inn, where Elsa will already have turned on the light. And you left. Yes. I left because the village could exist without me. If I stayed, I became the reason they pretended.

The reason to move signs and wait for inspections. The reason to say: see, Anna has come back. Giada walks slowly toward the houses while she talks. But you abandoned them. I left a place that wanted me as a symbol.

It is not the same thing. And the boy? I told him the truth one evening, before the water. That I was leaving, and that I was not coming back to become a story. Did he cry?

He said he understood, and maybe he did not. Then I took the workers' bus at dawn, as few people do. Giada stops near the fountain in the little square. So I am not here to close your circle. No.

You are Giada. If you stay for them, you repeat my mistake in reverse. If you run without understanding, you repeat my fear. Then what do I do? Understand why it still exists, and then leave.

Without becoming the girl in the register. Elsa comes out onto the inn threshold and looks at Giada on the phone. She is not calling you by chance. Giada raises a hand: wait. Mom, Elsa is here.

Tell her you are not me. I have been telling her for days. Then say it with your feet too: leave at dawn. The signal drops again, but this time Giada has enough. She ends the call and looks at Elsa without fresh hate.

My mother left a boy. He is not Neri's father. And she left because she wanted Collevento to exist without her. Elsa lowers her eyes, as if that sentence hurt her. Anna could have stayed.

She could, but she must not become your proof. Nor I. Elsa does not answer; she goes back in and leaves the door ajar. Giada stays in the square with her mother's voice still in her ears. The village existed before Anna, and it can exist without Giada.

That is the hard part to keep still. Do not tidy stories to make them fit. Do not make Neri the child of an old silence. Do not make yourself the daughter who repairs everything. Giada enters the inn, passes Elsa without fighting again, and goes upstairs.

In the room she opens the drawer and looks again at Anna's photo. Same jaw, same age, different story. She closes the drawer gently. Tomorrow, in daylight, she will go to the memorial. Not to look for ghosts.

To understand what remains when people leave. Giada thinks of the nameless boy Anna left. She does not try to invent a surname or a house in the square for him. She leaves that crooked piece, as Anna asked. Outside, someone still moves a pot for the inspection that does not come.

Inside, Giada better understands why her mother fled. It was not only finished love. It was the refusal to become proof and symbol. It was leaving the village free to exist without a girl at the center. Giada repeats softly: the village can exist without me.

The sentence does not erase the anger, but it gives it a direction. Do not tidy. Do not become Anna. Understand, then leave.

## Chapter 19: The forest by day (Nel bosco di giorno)

The next morning Giada goes out early, while the square is still quiet. She does not take Anna's bag; she takes only the phone and a water bottle. She wants to go to the forest by day, as she promised herself. Neri intercepts her near the fountain, with headphones around his neck. The forest again?

By day. I want to see the memorial without running. Neri hesitates, then walks with her for a stretch. There is nothing to win there. I know.

I am not looking for a ghost. The road toward the fence is narrow and full of stones. The air smells of damp earth and pine. Near the gate is the sign with the sinkhole pictogram. It is not a movie skull; it is a real worksite warning.

Tullio arrives from the path below, thermos in hand. By day you may look. At night no, and not because there are spirits. Because you cannot see the holes. Giada nods; this rule has physical sense.

Neri stays outside the gate, as if he already knew every name. I am not going in. All right. Giada opens the little gate and walks only where the grass is packed down. The memorial is a low stone slab, not a big monument.

On it there are names, dates, and a short sentence. Survey team, nineteen seventy-eight. She reads the surnames one by one, slowly, without photos. She does not want to turn respect into proof to collect. Some names are worn by rain; others still read clearly.

Behind the slab the ground drops with a dangerous edge. She stops at a distance; she does not go near the drop. She hears no voices, no music, no presence. She hears only the wind in the branches and her own breath. This is all.

An accident, a memory, a fence that asks for attention. Not a monster that explains the village. Giada rests a hand on the cold stone for a second. Then she takes it away. She does not pray; she does not invent a ceremony.

She only says, under her breath: thank you for the names. When she returns to the gate, Tullio looks at her without smiling. Did you see? I read. Good.

Reading is different from looking for ghosts. Neri walks with her again toward the village. People say things about the forest to keep children away. I know. I did not find a spirit.

I found a place that asks not to be used as proof. Neri raises an eyebrow. Proof of what? Of courage, of mystery, of a story bigger than us. Here the ground that gives way is enough.

They walk in silence for a few minutes. The high sun makes the village ordinary: laundry, dogs, a distant radio. Giada thinks that the fantastic, here, is remaining despite the maps. It is not another world. It is this world, kept off the timetables.

In front of the inn she stops. Thank you for walking with me. Neri shrugs, but he does not leave at once. If you leave, tell me. If you leave, tell your people.

Neri does not answer that sentence. He puts on the headphones and stays on the square bench. Giada goes up and washes her hands, as after serious work. In her head the names from nineteen seventy-eight remain. Not to solve them.

So as not to trample them while she looks for a way out. The forest by day did not give her a magic secret. It gave her a measure: respect, distance, exit. Later, from the window, she sees again the dark edge of the trees. She is not afraid of the forest as of another world.

She has respect for ground that can give way underfoot. The nineteen seventy-eight names stay in memory without theatricality. Giada does not use them to explain Elsa, nor the dam, nor her mother. She leaves them in their place: a memory of work gone wrong. She drinks water and washes the light mud from her shoes.

She thinks respect is also this: cleaning, not telling of spirits. In the square Neri listens to English music, as always. She does not ask him to enter the memorial with her another time. Everyone has their own boundaries around the village's pain. Giada chose to look by day, and to leave without ghosts in her pocket.

This choice feels more adult than yesterday's anger.

## Chapter 20: Choice (Scelta)

In the afternoon the engineer returns with the high car and the light shirt. He stops in the square as if he were a normal guest. Giada sees him from the inn window and goes down. Elsa is already outside, arms folded. Miss Rinaldi.

I need one simple thing. A photo of you here, with a date, and a short statement. That this place is inhabited. Giada looks at him without smiling. Why me, exactly?

Because you are not a resident of thirty years of confused papers. You are young, from outside, credible for a new project. If the place counts as inhabited, the numbers change. Compensation, priorities, deadlines. In other words: you become proof.

Elsa speaks before Giada answers. She is not your proof. She is Anna. She must stay here, with us. Giada feels two invisible hands pulling her opposite ways.

I am not Anna. Elsa takes a step forward. You have the same face, the same age, the same bag. If you stay, the village has a girl again at the center of the story. The engineer raises a peaceful hand.

I do not ask that she stay forever. I only ask for a useful signature. Then she can go to Trento with a safe ride. Neri arrives running from the street, without headphones. He heard the word ride.

If she leaves, take me too. I am not asking for money. I ask for a seat and a road that is not water. The square stays still: four people, three requests. The engineer wants inhabited proof.

Elsa wants Anna returned. Neri wants a ride out of the blank map. Giada feels the sun on her neck and the quiet anger return. You all want me to solve something. Nobody asks if I only want to reach my father.

The engineer smiles again, polite. The world works with documents. Elsa shakes her head. The world works with those who stay. Neri looks at Giada, not the others.

The world works if someone lets you get on. Giada takes a step back, toward the inn wall. I need to think. Do not sign anything today. Sign this evening, before the dam returns.

Just tell me if there is a seat. Giada goes in without answering any of the three. She goes up to her room and closes the door carefully. She sits on the bed and counts the requests on her fingers. One: be proof.

Two: be Anna. Three: be a taxi for Neri. None of the three is the girl she wants to become. She looks out: the engineer talks on the phone by the car. Elsa moves a pot in the square, a small staging gesture.

Neri smokes and watches the road that after sunset is useless. Giada opens the phone notes and writes three nos. Not yet said aloud, but already clear. I will not sign to make a project inhabited. I will not stay to fill my mother's place.

I will not take Neri away like a package if he does not speak with his people. The choice is not between staying and vanishing. It is between becoming the object of other people's stories or leaving with her own name. Giada turns off the screen and waits for the moment to say it. Under the window the three voices continue, low, like a market of needs.

She does not go down yet. First she must hold the answer steady. No. She goes down to the kitchen a moment only to drink water. Elsa does not speak; she arranges cups as if work could hold the whole village.

Giada does not apologize for not being Anna. She does not have to. She goes back up and rereads the three requests written on the phone. Proof. Anna. Ride. Each one seems reasonable from the mouth of the person who makes it.

Together they become a net to catch her face. She looks at Anna's bag on the floor and moves it under the bed. She does not want it to stay in sight like a costume ready to wear. Outside, the engineer's high car casts a shadow on the square. Neri waits for an answer she will not give in a hurry.

Elsa waits for a return that will not arrive. Giada waits only to say no with a steady voice. When the sun drops a little more, she goes down too.

## Chapter 21: No (No)

Toward evening Giada goes down to the square with the answer ready. The engineer is still there; Elsa on the threshold; Neri on the bench. Tullio watches from afar, like a human clock. I have decided. The engineer opens the folder with a smile already written.

No. I will not sign. I will not serve as proof that this place is inhabited for your project. The engineer's smile thins, but stays polite. It is a small favor.

Small for you, large for me. I do not lend my face to a paper that erases and rewrites villages. She turns toward Elsa. And no to you too. I am not Anna.

I will not stay to fill the empty place my mother left. Elsa opens her mouth and closes it again. If you leave, we are without again. Without Anna, maybe. But not without a village.

The village is you, even when nobody signs. Even when the inspection does not come. Elsa lowers her gaze to the stones of the square. Then Giada looks at Neri. You want a ride.

Yes. I will give it only if you ask your people. I will not take you away in secret like a rescued object. Neri stands up, nervous. If I ask, they tell me to stay.

Then stay, or leave after speaking. But do not use me as a shortcut. Neri looks at Elsa, then Tullio, then the ground. His mouth opens, and no question comes out. Not yet.

All right. Not today. Giada nods; it is not a triumph. It is a boundary held. The engineer closes the folder.

Without a signature the project proceeds anyway, more slowly. You have lost a clean opportunity. I kept my name. For me that is clean enough. The engineer gets into the high car before the shadow stretches too far.

He leaves toward the dam, where residents' rules do not hold him. Elsa stays on the threshold. So you are leaving. At dawn. Not after eight, and not as Anna.

Elsa makes a small gesture, almost a painful assent. Sleep here tonight. The room stays yours until morning. Thank you. Neri still does not ask his people.

He puts the headphones back on and walks toward the dam road, only to the safe point. Giada follows him with her eyes, without running after him. Tullio comes closer and speaks low. The workers' bus passes at dawn, if you are at the halt. It is not written on tourist timetables.

I know from my mother. Tullio nods, satisfied that the knowledge has an ordinary path. After dinner Giada packs the suitcase without theatrical hurry. She leaves Anna's photo in the drawer. It is not hers to take away as a trophy.

She takes only the bag she had already stolen in Bologna, and the suitcase. Before turning off the light she writes a message to Neri, even without network. If one day you ask, maybe there is still a road. Tonight no. Giada sleeps in stretches, awake at the idea of dawn.

The no said in the square stays in the room like a solid piece of furniture. It is not conquest of the village. It is the refusal to become the wrong story. In the night she still hears the distant sound of water on the road. She does not try to leave; she knows the hour.

She checks the alarm twice and prepares clothes for dawn. She thinks of Neri on the bench, with headphones and the unsaid question. She does not judge him. She knows how much it costs to ask your people to let you leave. But she cannot ask that question in his place.

She thinks of Elsa, who has perhaps already written Giada on the coffee note in her head. Or maybe she will write it only in the morning. She thinks of the engineer, already far away in a car that does not fear the water. The rules apply to those who stay; he visits and signs elsewhere. Giada closes her eyes with the three nos still clear.

Not proof. Not Anna. Not a ride without a question. At dawn she will use the rule the right way, as her mother did years ago. Exit, not conquer. Before dawn she hears light steps in the corridor: maybe Elsa checking the door. Nobody knocks.

The no said in the square has already done the hardest work. Now there is only getting up, walking, boarding the bus.

## Chapter 22: Dawn (L'alba)

Giada gets up when the sky is still light gray. In the kitchen Elsa has left coffee and bread, without a long speech. On the cloth there is a note: safe journey, Giada. The name is right, finally written without substitution. Giada drinks standing and takes the suitcase.

She goes out while Elsa is still sleeping, or pretends to sleep. The square is empty; last night's signs are in their wrong places. Giada does not fix them. It is not her job to save the staging. On the dam road the asphalt is wet but passable.

The evening water has gone down; puddles and the smell of mud remain. Tullio is already there, with the thermos and the clock in his head. You are on time. The water is down. At dawn, yes.

They walk together toward the crossed-out halt. Neri arrives from the other side of the road, hands in pockets. He has no suitcase. I am not getting on. I see that.

I have not asked yet. All right. Neri gives her a scrap of paper with a number written crookedly. If one day the signal works, and if I have asked. Giada puts the number in her pocket without promising miracles.

Thank you. At the halt the Collevento sign is still crossed out. Giada looks at it once, without touching it. A low gray bus arrives, with worksite men already seated. It is not a tourist train; it is a weekday ride.

The driver opens the door and looks at Giada without surprise. Sometimes someone from the village gets on. Tullio gives a short nod with his chin. That is all. There is no farewell speech, no blessing.

There is a nod that says: you know the rule and use it the right way. Giada boards with the suitcase and finds a seat by the window. The bus leaves at once, because the workers have a real timetable. Outside, Neri becomes small on the wet road. Tullio stays still, thermos in hand, like every useful dawn.

The village stays behind the bend, not conquered and not saved by a prophecy. Simply left in its place off the map. Giada does not cry. She watches the fields and counts the minutes toward a station that exists on timetables. In her pocket Neri's number stays folded, still unused.

In her head her mother's voice repeats: not after eight. She left earlier, the way you exit a rule you have finally understood. The bus goes down toward the valley and the light grows. A worker asks if she is going to Trento. Yes.

To my father's. The man nods and returns to the short morning sleep. Giada rests her forehead on the cold glass. Collevento did not defeat her and did not elect her. It asked her not to become Anna, and she answered by leaving.

This is the exit, not the conquest. When the bus reaches a real station, she buys an ordinary ticket. The name Trento is printed clear, with no black lines through it. Giada smiles a little, tired, and boards the right train. Behind her remains a village that does not exist on maps.

Ahead there is a kitchen, a father, and a story to tell without miracles. The train to Trento is full of ordinary people with ordinary suitcases. Giada rests her head on the seat and does not sleep at once. She thinks back to Tullio's nod, brief as a clock confirming the hour. She thinks of Neri still on the wet road, without a suitcase.

She thinks of the crossed-out sign left behind, not solved and not burned. The village will keep moving names for a maybe-future inspection. The dam will keep opening water on the road at a precise hour. She will not return after eight to prove something. She used dawn, the workers' bus, and her own name.

When the conductor passes, she shows a real ticket, printed clear. No black line through today's destination. Outside, stations pass that exist on timetables and maps. Giada watches them with a quiet hunger for verifiable things. Collevento stays a bend behind her back, not a victory in her pocket.

This is the exit she wanted: ordinary, on time, hers.

## Chapter 23: Trento (Trento)

In Trento her father waits under the station clock, not under a crossed-out sign. When he sees her, he raises a hand and that is enough. You are days late. I know. Let's go home; we eat; then you talk.

The apartment is small, tidy, with light on the pots. The kitchen smells of coffee and dish soap. Nothing is magical; everything is useful. Her father cuts the bread and pours the water. So.

Giada sits and holds her hands around the glass. I took the wrong train. I got off at Collevento. Her father furrows his brow. It does not exist on maps.

But it is there. A lake was planned for a dam; the papers erased it in advance. The people stayed. Every evening they open the water on the road. Her father listens without interrupting with theories.

Mom knew. She was there as a girl. She told me not to return after eight. Her father sets down the knife and stays still a moment. Anna never told it to me like that.

Maybe because it is not a miracle story. It is a story of timetables, papers, people waiting for inspections. And of an inn that wanted the girl from then back. Did they make you out to be her? Yes.

I said no. Her father nods, practical, almost relieved by the clarity. Good. You did not have to become your mother to be in the world. Giada eats a piece of bread and feels honest tiredness arrive.

There was also a boy, Neri. He wanted to leave. I did not take him away. Not yet. Why?

Because he had to ask his people, and he did not. Her father smiles a little, without giving a long lecture. You kept your head. I kept my name. Outside the window you see ordinary roofs and a piece of mountain.

No stage dam; no square to move for autumn. Her father washes two dishes and talks while the water runs. Sleep here today. Tomorrow we call your mother together, if you want. All right.

But I do not want to tell Collevento as a dream. Nor as a punishment. It was a real place with real rules. Her father dries his hands on the dish towel. Then we will keep it that way: true.

Giada stands and looks at the fridge, the calendar, the shopping list. This kitchen does not ask her to be anyone else. It only asks if she is hungry and if she has slept. Before going to her room, she takes out the phone. There is full signal, ordinary and wonderful.

She opens the photo gallery and finds the only image she kept. She does not show it to her father yet. The moment is not ready; or maybe she is, slowly. Good night, Dad. Good night.

In the guest room Giada sets down the suitcase and lies down dressed. She does not tell herself the story as a victory. She tells it as a successful exit. And as a family silence that finally got practical words. She closes her eyes with Trento around her, ordinary and enough.

In sleep she does not look for dams or registers; she only looks for rest. She wakes once, drinks water, and goes back to bed. In the kitchen her father leaves a low light, a parent habit. Giada thinks that telling without miracles is a way to respect the facts. The facts were: crossed-out halt, evening water, signs moved in the morning, memorial, refused signature.

The facts were also: Anna knew, Elsa wanted, Neri waited, Tullio nodded. None of these facts needs spirits to stand up. In the morning they will talk again, maybe with fewer words and more bread. For now the kitchen has done its job: receiving her without turning her into legend. Giada turns onto her side and feels the suitcase near the wall.

Inside there is still Anna's bag, but not Anna's whole story. There is her summer starting late, and that is fine. Trento does not ask for proof of habitation. It only asks if she closed the door and if she wants more water. Before falling asleep again she repeats to herself the sentence she told her father.

It was a real place with real rules. And she left with her own name, with no miracles to sell.

## Chapter 24: The photo (La foto)

The next day Giada has breakfast again in the kitchen, with her father reading his phone. Outside it rains gently on Trento; inside everything is warm and ordinary. Shall we open the photo? Giada hesitates, then nods. She shows the screen: the Collevento sign with the black line through the name.

No face, no square, no Elsa. Only the mark that someone wanted to erase a real place. Is this all? This is enough. In her head she puts a caption she does not write under the image.

It existed. It does not exist on the map. It does not exist on the tourist timetable. But it existed, and in a way it still exists, outside the lists. Her father looks for a long time, then gives the phone back.

Your mother should see this, one day. One day. Not today as an accusation. Later, as a proof of respect. They call Bologna in the afternoon.

Anna answers at once. Are you in Trento? Yes. I left at dawn. Anna takes a breath that sounds like real relief.

Thank you. Do not thank me for being myself. Anna is quiet a moment, then speaks softly. Thank you for not becoming my shadow. Giada looks at her father, who listens without stepping into the middle.

I have a photo of the sign. When you want, I will send it. Not today. All right. After the call Giada takes out Neri's scrap of paper.

The number is clear, written in a hurry. She saves it in contacts with one name only: Neri. She does not press call. She does not write a long message. The number stays unused, like a closed door with the key in the pocket.

It is not a betrayal. It is respect for the pact: he had to ask his people. Until he does, she does not open that road for him. Maybe a book later, or a summer later, that number will be useful. Now it serves to remember that exit is not a forced rescue.

Giada puts the phone on the table and helps her father with the dishes. They talk about shopping, work, a short trip to the mountains. Collevento does not occupy every sentence; it must not. It remains as a photo and a word: it existed. In the evening Giada looks at the image one more time.

The black line on the name no longer scares her as a riddle. It gives her respect as a fact. Someone erased a village on paper. People kept living there. She passed through, said no, and left at dawn.

She did not solve Collevento. She did not become Anna. She did not turn Neri into the abandoned boy of another summer. She kept the right boundaries. Before sleeping she writes three lines in the phone notes.

The village does not exist on the map. It existed. I am Giada. Then she turns everything off. The rain continues on Trento, ordinary.

In the pocket of the washed pants, Neri's number stays folded and quiet. Unused. Ready, if one day the question arrives in the right voice. For now Giada's summer can begin without conquest. Only with a photo, a kitchen, and a true word.

It existed. The next morning she will put the photo in a private phone folder. She will not publish it and will not use it as proof for anyone. It is a memory with a boundary, not a weapon. Her father makes coffee and asks if she wants eggs.

Yes. They eat without returning at once to Collevento. Sometimes respect is also this: not repeating the story at every meal. Giada checks contacts: Neri is there, silent. The finger does not press.

If one day he writes after asking his people, she will answer. If he does not write, the number will remain as an unforced possibility. Outside the rain lightens; the roofs shine. Giada washes the cup and thinks of the black line on the sign. A crossing-out does not remove existence from things; it only removes the official list.

She saw the place, said no, and left. Summer can continue without her solving the village. The village did not ask for a savior. It asked, at times, for a girl to put at the center. Giada refused the center and kept the photo.

It existed. And she still exists, with her own name, in Trento.
